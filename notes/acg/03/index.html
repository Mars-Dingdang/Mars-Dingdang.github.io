ACG 03: Transformations, Rotations, and Camera Frames

A transformation can move a point in one coordinate system or express the same point in another. In either case, name the source and destination frames and check the origin and basis vectors before composing matrices.

Affine transformations and homogeneous coordinates

With column vectors, the affine map \(x'=Ax+t\) becomes

\[\begin{pmatrix}x'\\1\end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix}A&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The difference of two point vectors has homogeneous component zero, so translations leave displacement vectors unchanged. A rotation around \(c\) is \(T(c)RT(-c)\), applied from right to left. The expression \(R(x-c)+c\) immediately verifies that the pivot remains fixed. For invertible \(A\), the inverse map is \(A^{-1}x'-A^{-1}t\).

Normal vectors follow a different rule. If \(n^\mathsf T u=0\) for a tangent \(u\), require \(n'^\mathsf T Au=0\). Choosing \(n'=A^{-\mathsf T}n\) preserves the dot product. Normalize afterward when a shader expects unit length. Using \(An\) works for rotations and uniform scaling up to normalization, but nonuniform scaling can break orthogonality. PBRT, “Transformations”

Rotations and the exponential map

A rotation preserves inner products, so \(R^\mathsf T R=I\). Its determinant is \(1\) because it also preserves orientation. These matrices form \(SO(3)\). Their inverses are transposes, and their products remain rotations.

Let \(u\) be a unit axis and set \(Kv=u\times v\). The triple product gives \(K^2=uu^\mathsf T-I\) and \(K^3=-K\). Summing the odd and even terms of the exponential power series yields Rodrigues’ formula,

\[R=e^{\theta K}=I+\sin\theta\,K+(1-\cos\theta)K^2.\]

In an orthonormal basis with \(u\) as one axis, \(R\) consists of a planar rotation block and an unchanged axis. Taking the trace gives \(\operatorname{tr}R=1+2\cos\theta\). Apply this identity to \(R_1^\mathsf T R_2\) to recover the angle between two orientations. The identity rotation has no unique axis. At angle \(\pi\), an axis and its negative define the same rotation.

Euler angles and quaternions

Euler angles depend on the rotation convention. For \(R=R_z(\gamma)R_y(\beta)R_x(\alpha)\), at \(\beta=\pi/2\) the \(x\) axis rotates onto \(-z\). Conjugating the rotation around \(x\) then gives

\[R_z(\gamma)R_y(\pi/2)R_x(\alpha)=R_z(\gamma-\alpha)R_y(\pi/2).\]

The two angles \(\gamma,\alpha\) no longer have independent effects. This is gimbal lock.

The unit quaternion \(q=(\cos(\theta/2),u\sin(\theta/2))\) rotates a vector through \((0,v')=q(0,v)q^{-1}\). Expanding the vector part gives

\[\cos\theta\,v+(1-\cos\theta)u(u^\mathsf Tv)+\sin\theta\,(u\times v),\]

the same Rodrigues formula. Since the signs cancel in the sandwich product, \(q\) and \(-q\) encode one rotation.

Implement shortest-arc spherical interpolation

Two unit quaternions span a two-dimensional plane in four dimensions. In that plane, the geodesic interpolation at fraction \(t\) combines them with sine weights. Flipping the endpoint representative when the dot product is negative chooses the shorter rotation path. The normalized linear limit avoids division by a tiny sine when the inputs are nearly equal.

The function assumes unit inputs, and the result represents \(q_0\) at \(t=0\) and the orientation of \(q_1\) at \(t=1\). When \(q_1=-q_0\), the entire interpolation represents the same orientation.

The view matrix

Let \(e\) be the camera center and let \(r,u,b\) denote its right, up, and backward unit vectors. Put the basis vectors into the columns of \(R\). The camera looks along its local negative \(z\) axis. Its camera-to-world map is \(p_w=Rp_c+e\), so

\[p_c=R^\mathsf T(p_w-e),\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}R^\mathsf T&-R^\mathsf T e\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

For target \(a\) and reference up vector \(u_0\), use \(b=\operatorname{normalize}(e-a)\), \(r=\operatorname{normalize}(u_0\times b)\), and \(u=b\times r\). A reference up parallel to \(b\) fails to determine the roll. The implementation should check that the camera center maps to the origin and that \(e-\lambda b\) with \(\lambda>0\) maps to negative local \(z\). The model-view composition is \(VM\), followed by the projection in Lecture 4.

Exercises

  1. Construct the correct normal. The plane \(x+y=0\) undergoes scaling by \(\operatorname{diag}(2,1,3)\). Derive its transformed equation and normal. Explain why applying the same matrix to the original normal disagrees.
Answer

Solve for the old coordinates to obtain \(x'/2+y'=0\). The new normal is proportional to \((1/2,1,0)\), given by the inverse transpose. Multiplying directly gives \((2,1,0)\), whose dot product with the transformed tangent \((2,-1,0)\) is \(3\).

  1. Move the model and camera together. Let a rigid motion \(T\) transform both the camera-to-world matrix \(C\) and the model-to-world matrix \(M\). Prove that camera-space geometry is invariant.
Answer

After the motion, the product is \((TC)^{-1}(TM)=C^{-1}T^{-1}TM=C^{-1}M\). The camera senses relative placement, so the common world-space motion cancels.

  1. Quaternion sign and timing. Interpolate from identity to a \(270^\circ\) rotation about \(z\) via shortest-arc SLERP. What orientation is represented at \(t=1/2\)?
Answer

The endpoint quaternion has negative real part \(\cos 135^\circ\). Its negation represents a \(-90^\circ\) turn about \(z\). The shortest arc begins at identity and ends at this signed angle, so its midpoint represents \(-45^\circ\) about \(z\).
