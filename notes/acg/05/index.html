ACG 05: Rasterization, Sampling, and Visibility

Rasterization converts projected geometry into samples on a finite grid. Triangle edge tests decide which samples are covered, filtering limits aliasing, and the depth buffer resolves opaque visibility at each sample.

Sampling a projected triangle

A pixel indexed by \((i,j)\) can be represented by the square \([i,i+1]\times[j,j+1]\). Center sampling tests the continuous triangle at \((i+\tfrac12,j+\tfrac12)\). A covered sample initially says nothing about the rest of the pixel. A triangle can cross a pixel without touching its center.

For counterclockwise vertices \(A,B,C\) in a coordinate system with \(y\) pointing upward, use the directed edge function

\[E_{AB}(P)=(B_x-A_x)(P_y-A_y)-(B_y-A_y)(P_x-A_x).\]

The function is a signed area scaled by two. The inside of a counterclockwise triangle lies to the left of all three oriented edges. Equivalently, \(E_{AB}(P)/E_{AB}(C)\) is the barycentric weight for \(C\), and the other two come from cyclic permutations. The three nonnegative weights sum to one and give a convex combination of the vertices.

Incremental edge evaluation

For fixed vertices, \(E(x,y)\) is affine. Moving one coordinate unit to the right changes it by \(-(B_y-A_y)\), and moving up changes it by \(B_x-A_x\). Initialize once at the first sample center of the triangle’s bounding box, cache a row start for each edge, and step across the row by addition. With an integer grid of \(S\) subpixel units per pixel, the increments for one pixel are \(-S(B_y-A_y)\) horizontally and \(S(B_x-A_x)\) vertically. Choose even \(S\) so pixel centers are representable and quantize shared vertices consistently.

In this \(y\)-up, counterclockwise convention, accept equality on an edge with \(B_y<A_y\), or on a horizontal edge with \(B_x<A_x\). A reversed shared edge has exactly the opposite tie classification. This fills the left and top boundaries consistently and leaves the bottom and right boundaries for their neighbors. With \(y\) pointing down, derive the rule again from the winding convention. A tile can be rejected when the maximum of an edge function over its four corners fails that edge test.

Why sampling aliases frequencies

Sampling step \(T\) is equivalent to multiplying by \(\sum_k\delta(t-kT)\). With the Fourier convention \(S(\nu)=\int s(t)e^{-2\pi i\nu t}dt\), the impulse train transforms into another impulse train at intervals \(1/T\) with nonzero weight \(1/T\). Multiplication in time becomes convolution in frequency, so

\[S_s(\nu)=\frac1T\sum_k S\left(\nu-\frac{k}{T}\right).\]

A band-limited signal with bandwidth \(B\) avoids overlap when \(1/T>2B\). Otherwise, distinct spectra may produce the same samples. The cosines at frequencies \(\nu\) and \(m/T-\nu\) agree at all sample times by periodicity and even symmetry. A triangle boundary creates a discontinuity, which has high-frequency content beyond any finite sample rate. Jagged edges and the apparent reversal of a filmed wheel are spatial and temporal examples of aliasing.

Prefiltering and multisampling

An ideal box average for a pixel region \(A\) and triangle \(T_\triangle\) is

\[c_A=\frac1{|A|}\int_A\mathbf 1_{T_\triangle}(x,y)\,dx\,dy =\frac{|A\cap T_\triangle|}{|A|}.\]

A constant foreground and a constant background can be blended in linear color space according to this coverage. With independent uniform subsamples, their average estimates \(c_A\) without bias, with variance \(c_A(1-c_A)/N\). A regular subsample grid is deterministic quadrature, so its error depends on alignment with the edge.

MSAA stores several coverage and depth samples while letting a shaded value serve multiple covered samples. It can smooth triangle edges without shading every subsample independently. High-frequency shading within a triangle retains its own sampling problem and can require texture filtering or per-sample shading.

Z-buffering

Start every depth sample at \(+\infty\) when smaller means nearer. Update the sample’s depth and color only if a new opaque fragment is closer. After any number of inputs, the buffer stores the minimum submitted depth: the empty minimum is \(+\infty\), and one update takes the minimum of the previous minimum and the arriving depth.

This invariant gives order independence for distinct opaque depths. Equal depths depend on the comparison and submission order. A global painter’s ordering can fail when three faces cyclically occlude one another. The Z-buffer resolves visibility locally, though overdraw can require testing many fragments per sample. Transparent fragments generally require combining several contributions and cannot use nearest-depth selection alone.

Reconstruction recap

The lecture first concludes its discussion of specialized reconstruction. In linear blend skinning, a bind-pose vertex \(v\) moves to \(\sum_j w_jG_j(\theta)G_j(0)^{-1}v\), where \(G_j\) is the global joint transform and the weights sum to one. At the bind pose each transform pair becomes identity, and the vertex stays fixed. To estimate the pose from one image, minimize projected joint error and regularize body shape and pose. Indoor reconstruction can estimate object shapes, placements, and room layout separately, then assemble them in one scene.

Exercises

  1. Prove shared-edge ownership. Two consistently counterclockwise triangles share an edge, and a sample is in the relative interior of that edge. Why does the fill rule assign it to exactly one triangle?
Answer

For a sample in the relative interior of a proper shared edge the other two edge tests of each triangle are positive. On the shared edge the tests are both zero. Its directed increments \((dx,dy)\) are opposite in the two triangles. If \(dy\ne0\), exactly one orientation has \(dy<0\). Otherwise \(dx\ne0\) and exactly one has \(dx<0\). Only that triangle accepts equality.

  1. Compute analytic antialiasing. A triangle covers the portion of the unit pixel where \(x+y<0.75\). Compare exact coverage with center sampling and a \(2\times2\) grid of quarter-offset samples.
Answer

Exact coverage is the right-triangle area \(0.75^2/2=0.28125\). The center has sum \(1\) and returns zero. The four grid sums are \(0.5,1,1,1.5\), giving estimated coverage \(0.25\).

  1. Find the alias. A signal \(\cos(2\pi\cdot 47t)\) is sampled at \(60\) Hz. Which nonnegative frequency in \([0,30]\) matches it, and can a post-sampling low-pass filter distinguish the two?
Answer

The aliased frequency is \(60-47=13\) Hz, since \(\cos(2\pi\cdot47k/60)=\cos(2\pi k-2\pi\cdot13k/60)\). Both continuous signals give exactly the same discrete input. A filter on that input has no evidence from which to distinguish their origins.
