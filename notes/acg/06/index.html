ACG 06: Shading, Texture Filtering, and Shadows

Shading computes the appearance of a visible sample. Its result depends on the local surface orientation, the attributes interpolated across the projected triangle, the area of the texture seen by the sample, and whether the light reaches the surface.

Lambert and Blinn–Phong

Let \(n\) be the unit normal, \(\ell\) the direction toward the source, and \(v\) the direction toward the viewer. A patch of physical area \(dA\) presents projected area \(dA\max(0,n\cdot\ell)\) to incoming light. For a point source with radiant intensity \(I\) in the direction toward the surface and distance \(r\), the resulting irradiance is

\[E=\frac I{r^2}\max(0,n\cdot\ell).\]

A Lambertian surface gives a direction-independent outgoing radiance \(kE\). The total outgoing flux per surface area is \(kE\int_{\Omega^+}\cos\theta\,d\omega=\pi kE\). Therefore a diffuse reflectance \(\rho\) gives \(k=\rho/\pi\). This normalization connects the classroom cosine term to a physically defined BRDF.

Blinn–Phong estimates a specular lobe via \(\max(0,n\cdot h)^p\), where \(h\) is the normalized \(\ell+v\) and the exponent \(p\) controls the lobe width. Local models can include ambient light as a constant approximation to unresolved transport. Flat shading uses one normal per face. Gouraud shading interpolates colors computed at the vertices and can miss a highlight entirely if it lies between them. Phong shading interpolates normals, renormalizes them, and evaluates lighting per fragment.

Perspective-correct interpolation

Consider clip positions \(c_i\) with fourth components \(w_i\) and affine weights \(\mu_i\) before projection. For the covered screen point, the perspective divide gives

\[\frac{\sum_i\mu_i(c_i)_{xy}}{\sum_i\mu_iw_i} =\sum_i\frac{\mu_iw_i}{\sum_j\mu_jw_j} \frac{(c_i)_{xy}}{w_i}.\]

By uniqueness of barycentric coordinates, screen weights \(\lambda_i\) are \(\mu_iw_i/\sum_j\mu_jw_j\). Solving for the original weights gives the rule for vertex attribute \(a\),

\[a=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i a_i/w_i}{\sum_i\lambda_i/w_i}.\]

In a rasterizer, compute \(a_i/w_i\) and \(1/w_i\) at the vertices, interpolate both independently in screen space, then take the quotient. In texture coordinates, this produces the correct texture motion when a surface tilts relative to the camera. UV seams may duplicate a geometric vertex in the attribute buffer so adjacent triangles can use different UV values.

Filtering textures

Magnification calls for reconstruction between texels. With fractional offsets \(s,t\in[0,1]\), bilinear interpolation produces

\[c(s,t)=(1-t)\left[(1-s)c_{00}+sc_{10}\right] +t\left[(1-s)c_{01}+sc_{11}\right].\]

Minification calls for an average across the texture footprint. A mipmap filters and halves both texture dimensions at each level. The full chain for a large square power-of-two texture approaches \(1+1/4+1/16+\cdots=4/3\) times the base storage.

Select a mip level from derivatives

Estimate texel-space footprint vectors from the derivatives of normalized UV coordinates. Convert normalized coordinates to texel units with the base width and height before taking vector lengths.

A derivative magnitude of one texel per screen pixel uses level zero. A magnitude of eight selects level three. Averaging neighboring mip levels reduces transitions between discrete estimates. An elongated anisotropic footprint still calls for more sampling along its long axis than along its short axis. Isotropic mip selection based on the larger length can blur details across the short axis.

Texture channels may also store normals, volume densities, or displacements. For a local height field \((u,v,h(u,v))\), crossing the two partial derivatives gives the normal \((-h_u,-h_v,1)\). Bump and normal mapping change the direction used for shading. Displacement moves surface positions and therefore can change the silhouette or cast shadows.

Two-pass shadow mapping

First, rasterize from the light and store the nearest depth for each sample. Then rasterize from the eye. Project each camera-visible fragment into the light’s clip coordinates, divide by its fourth component, and compare the result with the shadow depth in that light-space sample. If the query is farther by more than a tolerance, a surface lies between it and the source. Too little bias reveals artifacts from finite precision and resolution. Too much moves the shadow away from its caster. An area light requires integrating visibility over its surface; one shadow view samples only one position.

Exercises

  1. Quantify interpolation drift. Triangle vertices have clip \(w=(1,2,4)\), scalar attributes \((0,1,1)\), and screen weights \((1/2,1/4,1/4)\). Calculate the correct scalar and the screen-linear error.
Answer

The denominator is \(1/2+1/8+1/16=11/16\). The numerator is \(1/8+1/16=3/16\), giving \(3/11\). The direct estimate is \(1/2\), an overestimate of \(5/22\).

  1. Select the filtering footprint. For a \(1024\times512\) base map, normalized derivatives along screen \(x\) and \(y\) are \((1/256,0)\) and \((0,1/64)\). Find the mip level and estimate the ratio between the two footprint axes.
Answer

The vectors in texel units are \((4,0)\) and \((0,8)\), so max-length mip selection yields \(\log_2 8=3\). These orthogonal footprint axes are in a ratio of \(2:1\). An isotropic level covers approximately eight texels on both axes and filters across twice the short-axis length.

  1. Compare diffuse normalization. A surface with \(\rho=0.7\) receives irradiance \(E=10\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\). Compare outgoing radiance and total flux per unit area for the expressions \(\rho E\) and \(\rho E/\pi\) interpreted as radiance.
Answer

The normalized radiance is \(7/\pi\,\mathrm{W/(m^2\,sr)}\) and its hemisphere integral is \(7\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\). Taking the radiance as \(7\,\mathrm{W/(m^2\,sr)}\) yields \(7\pi\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\), more than twice the incident power density.
