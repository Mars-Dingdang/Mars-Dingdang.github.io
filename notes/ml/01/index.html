ML 01: Supervised Learning, Risk, and Data

Derive the optimal predictions under squared and classification loss, then trace how training data, validation, and deployment define different risks.

The training samples contain noise. The test curve compares predictions on a dense grid with the noiseless function.

Empirical and population risk

Given independent draws \(S=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) from a distribution \(P\), a loss \(\ell\), and a hypothesis class \(\mathcal H\), empirical risk minimization solves

\[\widehat h\in\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal H}\widehat R_S(h), \qquad \widehat R_S(h)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(h(x_i),y_i).\]

The population risk is \(R(h)=\mathbb E_P[\ell(h(X),Y)]\). For a fixed predictor independent of \(S\), \(\mathbb E_S[\widehat R_S(h)]=R(h)\) by linearity. The model \(\widehat h(S)\) depends on the sample. For every fixed \(h\), \(\mathbb E\widehat R_S(\widehat h)\le\mathbb E\widehat R_S(h)=R(h)\), while \(\mathbb E R(\widehat h)\ge\inf_{h\in\mathcal H}R(h)\). Fitting and evaluating on the same data can therefore produce an optimistic estimate. For example, a flexible model can memorize independent fair-coin labels on distinct training inputs, while any prediction for an independent input is correct with probability \(1/2\).

Optimal prediction depends on the loss

Write \(m(x)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]\). Expanding the squared error about \(m(x)\) kills its cross term after conditioning:

\[\mathbb E[(Y-a)^2\mid X=x]=\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x)+(m(x)-a)^2.\]

The conditional mean minimizes squared loss. For absolute loss, the left and right derivatives of the conditional objective are \(2P(Y<a\mid x)-1\) and \(2P(Y\le a\mid x)-1\). Their interval contains zero precisely at a conditional median.

For binary classification let \(\eta(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x)\). Predicting 0 incurs conditional error \(\eta(x)\) and predicting 1 incurs \(1-\eta(x)\), so

\[h^\star(x)=\mathbf1\{\eta(x)\ge\tfrac12\},\qquad R(h^\star)=\mathbb E\min\{\eta(X),1-\eta(X)\}.\]

With false-positive cost \(c_{\rm FP}\) and false-negative cost \(c_{\rm FN}\), compare \(c_{\rm FP}(1-\eta)\) and \(c_{\rm FN}\eta\). The optimal positive threshold is \(c_{\rm FP}/(c_{\rm FP}+c_{\rm FN})\).

Choosing a model and estimating its performance

The training set determines parameters. A validation set chooses polynomial degree, regularization strength, or training duration. Once these choices are fixed, an independent test set estimates performance. In \(k\)-fold cross-validation, fit on \(k-1\) folds and predict on the omitted fold, repeating until every example has a held-out prediction. Fit standardization and feature selection inside each training fold. Group related measurements by entity and split forecasting data by time. Otherwise the evaluation can reuse information that would be unavailable at prediction time.

For equal-size folds and a fixed learning rule, the expectation of the cross-validation score equals the expected risk of that rule trained on \(n(1-1/k)\) examples. To see this, condition on the complementary training data for each fold. Its held-out mean is then an unbiased estimate of the fitted predictor’s population risk. The complement itself is an independent training sample of the stated size. Linearity gives the risk identity after averaging. Different fitted models reuse training cases, so their scores are generally dependent. Selecting the learning rule based on those scores also changes the interpretation of the selected score.

Increasing polynomial degree enlarges the hypothesis class, so training error cannot increase: the previous solution remains feasible. Test error can rise as the model becomes more sensitive to sample noise. An explicit tradeoff is \(\min_w\widehat R_S(h_w)+\lambda\|w\|_2^2\). The optimizer and the statistical guarantees for such objectives are developed in Lecture 2 and Lecture 5.

Dataset construction and unlabeled data

Labeling pipelines can separate candidate selection, annotation, and verification. For object masks, a disagreement between annotators can reflect ambiguity in the target definition. The sampling plan should reflect the deployment distribution, including entities and acquisition conditions. Clustering, PCA, and generative modeling infer structure from \(X\). Semi-supervised methods add information when a relationship between \(P_X\) and \(P(Y\mid X)\) is assumed. For example, a decision boundary might pass through regions of low input density. Holding \(P_X\) fixed leaves many possible label rules, all consistent with the same unlabeled observations.

ImageNet begins with WordNet synonym sets, retrieves candidate images using search terms and translations, then calibrates worker consensus against validation examples. The consensus threshold can differ between categories because ambiguous synsets elicit different vote distributions. COCO instead recruits ordinary scenes with multiple objects and separates category-presence labeling, instance spotting, mask tracing, and verification. Pooling presence votes by union is useful for recall: if an object is independently missed with probability \(q\) by each of \(m\) annotators, everyone misses it with probability \(q^m\). The same union also admits an independently generated false positive with probability \(1-(1-p)^m\), where \(p\) is the per-worker false-positive probability. The arithmetic explains why independent verification has a different function from candidate discovery. Deng et al., ImageNet, §3 and Fig. 3, Lin et al., Microsoft COCO, §4 and Figs. 3–4.

When can unlabeled inputs help?

Let \(X\) be uniform on two disjoint regions \(A,B\) and suppose labels are constant on each region. From one labeled point in \(A\), the same \(P_X\) and observed label are compatible with either choice of label on \(B\). For a learner given arbitrary unlabeled data, average its conditional errors on \(B\) over these two indistinguishable labelings. At each point in \(B\), one of the two labels disagrees with its prediction, so the mean error contribution from \(B\) alone is \(P_X(B)/2\). If an extra assumption says the two regions must have opposite labels, the observation in \(A\) determines the label throughout \(B\). The drawings comparing two labeled points with two labeled points plus many unlabeled ones illustrate the latter kind of information, with the cluster geometry inferred from the unlabeled sample.

A worked sampling model

The course’s exam-design example estimates a student’s mean success probability across a pool of possible questions. For a uniform independent draw of questions and independent responses conditional on the chosen questions, the observed scores \(B_i\) are Bernoulli with marginal mean \(p=\mathbb E_q p(q)\). Thus \(\widehat p=n^{-1}\sum_iB_i\) has expectation \(p\) and variance \(p(1-p)/n\). If question selection places unequal weight on topics, that mean changes accordingly. This is the same sampling issue as evaluating a predictor under a shifted input distribution. The accompanying preference survey converts each respondent’s five ratings \(v_i\) to proportions \(r_i=(v_i-1)/\sum_j(v_j-1)\) before averaging. For any respondent with at least one rating above one, these coordinates sum to one. This gives respondents equal total weight. Normalizing only after summing gives respondent \(j\) weight proportional to \(\sum_i(v_{ji}-1)\), a different statistic.

A Chernoff calculation for demand

For independent \(X_i\in[0,1]\), let \(p_i=\mathbb E X_i\) and \(Z=\sum_iX_i\) with mean \(\mu\). By convexity, \(e^{tx}\le 1+x(e^t-1)\) on \([0,1]\), with equality at both endpoints. Therefore, for \(t>0\),

\[\mathbb E e^{tZ}\le\prod_i(1+p_i(e^t-1))\le \exp(\mu(e^t-1)).\]

By Markov’s inequality, \(P(Z\ge(1+a)\mu)\le e^{\mu(e^t-1)-t(1+a)\mu}\). The minimizing \(t=\log(1+a)\) gives \(\exp(-\mu[(1+a)\log(1+a)-a])\). For \(a\in[0,1]\), \(\log(1+a)\ge 2a/(2+a)\ge 2a/3\). The first inequality follows by differentiating the difference, whose derivative is \(a^2/((1+a)(2+a)^2)\ge0\), and using equality at zero. Integrating gives \((1+a)\log(1+a)-a\ge a^2/3\). For 15 independent servers with total mean 6, each receives a request with probability \(0.4\). Overload at 10 requests corresponds to \(a=2/3\), so \(P(Z\ge10)\le e^{-8/9}\approx0.411\).

Challenges

Problem 1

Show that the excess 0–1 risk of a plug-in classifier \(\widehat h=\mathbf1\{\widehat\eta\ge1/2\}\) is at most \(2\mathbb E|\widehat\eta(X)-\eta(X)|\). If \(\|\widehat\eta-\eta\|_\infty\le a\) and \(P(|\eta(X)-1/2|\le t)\le Ct^\kappa\), strengthen the rate.

Show solution

At a point of disagreement with the Bayes rule, the additional conditional error is \(|2\eta(x)-1|\). Disagreement implies \(|\eta(x)-1/2|\le|\widehat\eta(x)-\eta(x)|\), because the two scores straddle the threshold. Take expectations. Under the uniform bound, all disagreements lie in the band of width \(a\) and their conditional cost is at most \(2a\), so the excess risk is at most \(2Ca^{\kappa+1}\).

Problem 2

Given \(M\) candidate predictors trained independently of a validation set of \(n\) examples with losses in \([0,1]\), give a high-probability bound on the selected candidate’s excess risk. What role does independence play?

Show solution

Conditioned on the candidates, Hoeffding and a union bound put all empirical risks within \(\varepsilon/2\) of their true risks except with probability \(2M e^{-n\varepsilon^2/2}\). The selected function then has excess risk at most \(\varepsilon\) by two applications of the deviation bound and empirical optimality. Thus \(n\ge 2\log(2M/\delta)/\varepsilon^2\) suffices. Adaptive candidate construction changes the law of the validation sample conditional on those functions, so the fixed-candidate concentration need not apply.

Problem 3. From pointwise estimation to fast classification rates

Let \(D(x)=|\eta(x)-1/2|\). Suppose \(P_X(0<D(X)\le t)\le Ct^\kappa\) and a random regression estimator obeys \(P_{\rm train}(|\widehat\eta(x)-\eta(x)|\ge t)\le A e^{-bt^2}\) for \(P_X\)-almost every fixed \(x\) and \(0<t\le1/2\), with \(b\ge4\). Prove that the expected excess risk of the plug-in classifier is \(O(b^{-(\kappa+1)/2})\). This is the regression-to-classification step in the fast rates for plug-in rules. Audibert and Tsybakov, Fast Learning Rates for Plug-in Classifiers, Lemma 3.1.

Show solution

Conditional on \(x\), disagreement with the Bayes rule contributes \(2D(x)\) and requires estimation error at least \(D(x)\). The set \(D=0\) contributes zero. Put \(r=b^{-1/2}\) and split the remaining inputs into \(0<D\le r\) and shells \(2^{j-1}r<D\le2^jr\), \(j\ge1\). The first region contributes at most \(2Cr^{\kappa+1}\). On shell \(j\), the probability of disagreement is at most \(A e^{-4^{j-1}}\) and the shell’s mass is at most \(C(2^jr)^\kappa\). The contribution is bounded by \(2ACr^{\kappa+1}2^{j(\kappa+1)}e^{-4^{j-1}}\). For every nonempty shell its lower threshold is at most \(1/2\), so the stated tail assumption suffices. The exponential makes the shell series summable for each fixed \(\kappa\). Substituting \(r\) proves the rate, without a uniform-in-\(x\) concentration assumption.
