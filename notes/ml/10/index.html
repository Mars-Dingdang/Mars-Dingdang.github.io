ML 10: K-means and Spectral Clustering

Derive Lloyd’s updates, prove the graph Laplacian’s spectral properties, and obtain a clustering algorithm by relaxing RatioCut.

The bridge is weak relative to the triangle edges. The second eigenvector places the two triangles on opposite sides of zero.

Lloyd’s alternating updates

K-means minimizes \(\sum_i\|x_i-\mu_{c_i}\|^2\) over assignments and centers. With centers fixed, each point chooses its closest center independently. For a fixed nonempty cluster \(S\), expansion about its mean gives

\[\sum_{i\in S}\|x_i-\mu\|^2 =\sum_{i\in S}\|x_i-\bar x_S\|^2+|S|\|\mu-\bar x_S\|^2.\]

The cross term vanishes because the centered points sum to zero. Therefore the optimal center is the cluster mean. Exact alternating updates cannot increase the objective. If assignments change only on strict improvements and empty centers are held fixed, the finite number of partitions ensures eventual stability. The solution can depend on initialization. In Euclidean space, nearest centers induce convex Voronoi cells, which need not follow a curved cluster.

Graph Laplacian energy and null space

Let \(W\) be a symmetric nonnegative weight matrix, \(D_{ii}=\sum_jW_{ij}\), and \(L=D-W\). Symmetry gives

\[u^\top Lu=\sum_iD_{ii}u_i^2-\sum_{ij}W_{ij}u_iu_j =\tfrac12\sum_{ij}W_{ij}(u_i-u_j)^2.\]

Thus \(L\) is positive semidefinite. The energy is zero exactly when every edge of positive weight joins two equal coordinates. This means \(u\) is constant on each connected component, so the multiplicity of eigenvalue zero is the number of components. In particular, \(L\mathbf1=0\).

RatioCut as a relaxation

Define \(W(A,B)=\sum_{i\in A,j\in B}W_{ij}\). For a partition \(A,B\), take \(u_i=\sqrt{|B|/(n|A|)}\) on \(A\) and \(u_i=-\sqrt{|A|/(n|B|)}\) on \(B\). Then \(\|u\|=1\) and \(u\perp\mathbf1\). All internal edges have zero energy. A crossing edge contributes its weight times \((1/|A|+1/|B|)\), so \(u^\top Lu=\operatorname{RatioCut}(A,B)\). Removing the two-level constraint permits any unit vector perpendicular to \(\mathbf1\). Expand in an eigenbasis with \(\mathbf1/\sqrt n\) first. The minimum is \(\lambda_2(L)\), achieved by a Fiedler vector, and is a lower bound on the discrete optimum. A threshold or one-dimensional two-means converts its real coordinates into a cut.

For \(k\) nonempty groups, set \(H_{ij}=\mathbf1\{i\in A_j\}/\sqrt{|A_j|}\). Then \(H^\top H=I\) and

\[\operatorname{tr}(H^\top LH)=\sum_j\frac{W(A_j,\overline{A_j})}{|A_j|}.\]

To solve the relaxation over all \(H^\top H=I\), let \(u_i\) be Laplacian eigenvectors and write the trace as \(\sum_i\lambda_i c_i\) where \(c_i=\|H^\top u_i\|^2\). Since \(HH^\top\) is a rank-\(k\) orthogonal projector, \(0\le c_i\le1\) and \(\sum_i c_i=k\). Moving weight from a larger eigenvalue to a smaller one until the smallest \(k\) have weight one gives the lower bound \(\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i\). Taking their eigenvectors as columns attains it. This is the trace minimization proof used for the spectral embedding; each row is a node’s embedding, and k-means rounds the points to clusters. The same trace argument, applied to the largest eigenvalues, gives the PCA characterization from Lecture 9.

Replacing the size of a group by its total degree gives normalized cut. Its relaxed matrix on positive-degree vertices is \(I-D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}\), obtained by substituting \(D^{1/2}u\) into a Rayleigh quotient with denominator \(u^\top Du\). Neighborhood definition and edge weights determine the graph; their scale affects both connectedness and the distinction between weak and strong edges.

The Birkhoff proof in the lecture

Complete \(H\) to an orthogonal basis and express its columns in an eigenbasis of \(L\), obtaining an orthogonal matrix \(Q\). The matrix \(B\) with entries \(Q_{ij}^2\) is doubly stochastic because each row and column of \(Q\) has squared norm one. By Birkhoff’s theorem, \(B\) is a convex combination of permutation matrices. The objective is the linear expression \(\sum_i\lambda_i\sum_{j\le k}B_{ij}\). At each permutation this is the sum of some \(k\) distinct eigenvalues, at least \(\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_k\). The same is true of their convex combination. The first \(k\) eigenvectors give equality.

Two supplementary proofs of the trace minimum

PCA and best low-rank approximation. Choose \(c>\lambda_{\max}(L)\) and set \(A=cI-L=XX^\top\) using the symmetric square root. Minimizing \(\operatorname{tr}(H^\top LH)\) over \(H^\top H=I_k\) is equivalent to maximizing \(\operatorname{tr}(H^\top XX^\top H)\), since their sum is \(ck\). For a fixed column subspace, the best approximation of \(X\) in that subspace is the projection \(HH^\top X\). Its squared error is \(\|X\|_F^2-\operatorname{tr}(H^\top XX^\top H)\). Every matrix of rank at most \(k\) has columns in such a subspace, so the problem is exactly best rank-\(k\) approximation.

Here is the singular value argument from Zeyang Zhang’s supplement. For \(n\)-column matrices \(B,C\), take the subspaces orthogonal to the leading \(i-1\) right singular vectors of \(B\) and leading \(j-1\) of \(C\). Their intersection has dimension at least \(n-i-j+2\), and \(\|(B+C)v\|\le(\sigma_i(B)+\sigma_j(C))\|v\|\) there. The variational formula for singular values, which minimizes the maximal stretch over subspaces of this dimension, gives \(\sigma_{i+j-1}(B+C)\le\sigma_i(B)+\sigma_j(C)\). This variational formula follows by choosing the trailing right singular vectors for the upper bound and intersecting any candidate subspace with the span of the first \(i+j-1\) right singular vectors for the lower bound. Set \(B=X-M\), \(C=M\), and \(j=k+1\). Since \(\sigma_{k+1}(M)=0\) for rank at most \(k\), summing squares gives

\[\|X-M\|_F^2=\sum_i\sigma_i(X-M)^2\ge\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i(X)^2.\]

The rank-\(k\) truncated singular value decomposition attains the bound. The largest eigenvalues of \(XX^\top=cI-L\) correspond to the smallest ones of \(L\), giving the desired trace minimum.

Lagrange multipliers and the determinant argument. This follows Wenjie Huang’s supplement. For any symmetric \(A\), use the Lagrangian \(\operatorname{tr}(H^\top AH)-\operatorname{tr}(M(H^\top H-I))\), with \(M\) symmetric. The derivative of the constraint is onto the symmetric \(k\times k\) matrices, because the perturbation \(HS/2\) gives derivative \(S\) for any symmetric \(S\). Thus at a minimizer \(AH=HM\). To see the determinant step, form the block matrix \(D\) with commuting entries \(D_{ij}=\delta_{ij}A-M_{ij}I\). The cofactor identity \(\operatorname{adj}(D)D=\det(D)I\) holds over the commutative ring generated by \(A\) and \(I\): an off-diagonal entry is the expansion of a determinant with two equal columns and vanishes. Applying it to the column vectors of \(H\) gives \(p(A)h_j=0\) with \(p(t)=\det(tI_k-M)\).

For simple spectrum, \(p(A)\) has at least \(k\) linearly independent null vectors, so \(p(t)\) vanishes at \(k\) distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_{i_1},\ldots,\lambda_{i_k}\) of \(A\). Since \(p\) is monic of degree \(k\), comparing the coefficient of \(t^{k-1}\) gives \(\operatorname{tr}(M)=\sum_{j=1}^k\lambda_{i_j}\). The objective is \(\operatorname{tr}(M)\) at the stationary point and is at least the sum of the smallest \(k\) eigenvalues. Choosing their eigenvectors attains equality. For repeated eigenvalues diagonalize \(M=Q\Lambda Q^\top\): \(AHQ=HQ\Lambda\), so the \(k\) orthonormal columns select eigenvalues with multiplicity at most their eigenspace dimensions. The same lower bound follows. Compactness of \(H^\top H=I\) ensures the minimum is attained.

Challenge

Suppose \(G\) has exactly \(k\) connected components. Show that k-means on the rows of any orthonormal basis of the bottom-\(k\) eigenspace has a unique zero-loss grouping up to label order.

Show solution

The normalized component indicators form an orthonormal basis \(B\) of the null space. Every other orthonormal basis is \(BQ\) for an orthogonal \(Q\). The rows of \(B\) are constant within each component, and different components use nonzero multiples of distinct standard basis vectors, so have distinct rows. Orthogonal rotation preserves these distances. There are exactly \(k\) distinct embedded points. A zero-loss solution requires all rows assigned to a center to be identical, so its \(k\) centers must be the \(k\) distinct points and recover the components.

Second challenge. For a connected weighted graph, show the normalized Laplacian has spectrum in \([0,2]\). When does the largest eigenvalue reach 2?

Show solution to the second challenge

For \(v=D^{-1/2}u\), the energy is \(\tfrac12\sum_{ij}W_{ij}(v_i-v_j)^2\). The bound \((a-b)^2\le2(a^2+b^2)\) makes it at most \(2\sum_iD_{ii}v_i^2=2\|u\|^2\). Equality requires \(v_i=-v_j\) on every positive-weight edge, so a connected graph has a nonzero equality vector exactly when its edges respect a bipartition. On a bipartite graph take \(v=1\) on one side and \(-1\) on the other.
