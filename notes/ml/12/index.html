ML 12: Robust Learning and Randomized Smoothing

Derive exact robust margins for linear classifiers and prove a Gaussian-smoothing certificate for bounded input changes.

A shift in the input changes the probability assigned to either side of the classifier’s decision boundary.

Robust risk and the envelope derivative

Lecture 12, slides 4–21. The examples motivate measuring model behavior under bounded input changes as well as accuracy on unchanged inputs.

A perturbation set \(\Delta\) models allowable input changes. The worst-case training objective is

\[\min_\theta\mathbb E_{X,Y}\!\left[\max_{\delta\in\Delta}\ell(f_\theta(X+\delta),Y)\right].\]

An \(\ell_\infty\) ball bounds each coordinate change, whereas an \(\ell_2\) ball bounds total squared magnitude. Robustness depends on the selected input metric.

For a differentiable inner loss \(g_\theta(\delta)=\ell(f_\theta(x+\delta),y)\), its first-order change is \(\nabla_\delta g_\theta(0)^\top\delta\). On the box \(\|\delta\|_\infty\le r\), each coordinate contributes at most \(r|\partial_i g_\theta(0)|\), attained by choosing that coordinate’s sign to match the derivative. This derives the first-order sign rule on slide 11. Its value is exact for the linearized loss. Projected ascent updates a current \(\delta\) and projects it back to \(\Delta\). For the box, the squared Euclidean projection objective separates by coordinates, and each minimizer is a clipping operation. With a nonconcave loss, this generally solves only a local problem.

Let \(F(\theta)=\max_{z\in\Delta}g(\theta,z)\), with \(\Delta\) compact and \(g,\nabla_\theta g\) continuous. Write \(M(\theta)\) for its set of maximizers. For any \(z\in M(\theta)\), evaluating it at \(\theta+tv\) gives the lower bound \(\liminf_{t\downarrow0}(F(\theta+tv)-F(\theta))/t\ge \nabla_\theta g(\theta,z)^\top v\). For the upper bound choose a maximizer \(z_t\) at \(\theta+tv\). Since \(F(\theta)\ge g(\theta,z_t)\), the difference quotient is bounded above by the directional change in \(g\) at \(z_t\). Compactness gives a convergent subsequence of maximizers, continuity puts its limit in \(M(\theta)\), and the uniform first-order remainder yields

\[F'(\theta;v)=\max_{z\in M(\theta)}\nabla_\theta g(\theta,z)^\top v.\]

For a unique maximizer \(z_\star\), the gradient of \(F\) is \(\nabla_\theta g(\theta,z_\star)\). For differentiability, take \(h\to0\). By compactness and uniqueness every maximizing sequence \(z_{\theta+h}\) tends to \(z_\star\). The first-order expansions at \(z_\star\) and \(z_{\theta+h}\) sandwich \(F(\theta+h)-F(\theta)\) between two linear terms differing by \(o(\|h\|)\). Several maximizers can make the directional derivative nonlinear in \(v\), as for \(\max(\theta,-\theta)\) at zero.

For a minibatch, compute an approximate inner maximizer for each example and differentiate the outer average at the selected inputs. The exact envelope identity is a statement about true inner maximizers. Each feasible input evaluated during a numerical search gives a lower bound on worst-case loss. In accuracy, a finite check gives an upper bound on worst-case accuracy, while prediction invariance gives the lower bound used for certification. If unlike labels have overlapping perturbation sets, one deterministic classifier cannot give the correct label throughout both sets. For instance, two points at \(\ell_p\) distance at most \(2r\) share their midpoint inside both radius-\(r\) sets.

Linear robust margins are exact

For a linear score \(w^\top x+b\) and \(y\in\{-1,1\}\), let \(m=y(w^\top x+b)\). The dual-norm identity gives

\[\min_{\|\delta\|_p\le r}y(w^\top(x+\delta)+b)=m-r\|w\|_q,\quad 1/p+1/q=1.\]

Hölder’s inequality gives the lower bound, and compactness of the unit ball and the definition of the dual norm give equality. The whole closed ball is classified correctly when \(m>r\|w\|_q\). At \(p=2\) the limiting radius agrees with the geometric distance from the SVM lecture.

Evaluating robustness with nonsmooth or random components

Lecture 12, slides 19–28. Athalye et al., Sections 2–4.

An inaccurate or uninformative gradient can make a numerical lower bound particularly loose. The slides distinguish shattered gradients from nonsmooth computations, stochastic gradients from randomized transformations, and badly scaled gradients through long chains of operations. A bounded prediction check measures the full forward model. In a differentiable approximation to a composition, every replaced derivative changes the product of Jacobians. Through randomness, differentiating an expected loss gives the expected derivative when a local integrable bound justifies differentiation under the integral. A single random gradient need not characterize the expected model.

The Gaussian smoothing proof

Lecture 12, slides 29–37. Cohen, Rosenfeld, and Kolter, Section 3 and Appendix A.

For base classifier \(f\), smooth by class probabilities \(p_c(x)=P(f(x+Z)=c)\) with \(Z\sim N(0,\sigma^2 I)\) and choose the most likely class. Suppose the leading class \(A\) has probability at least \(\underline p_A\) at \(x\) and any other class has probability at most \(\overline p_B<\underline p_A\).

Greedy filling for a general noise kernel

The slides start with a binary problem. Let \(E=\{z:f(z)=A\}\) be the region assigned to the leading class, and let \(P,Q\) be the laws of noisy inputs at \(x\) and \(x+\delta\). Given only the histogram entry \(P(E)=a\), seek the smallest possible \(Q(E)\). In a discretized space with cell weights \(p_i,q_i\), choose fractions \(0\le u_i\le1\) of cells assigned to \(A\) to minimize \(\sum_i q_i u_i\) subject to \(\sum_i p_i u_i=a\). A cell with \(p_i=0<q_i\) can be left unfilled. Among the others, if a cell with ratio \(q_i/p_i\) larger than another is filled before that cheaper one is full, transfer a small amount of \(P\)-mass to the cheaper cell. The change in \(Q\)-mass is that amount times the difference of ratios, and is negative. Thus the optimum fills in increasing \(Q/P\) order, taking a fraction of a threshold cell if necessary. Slide 36 uses the reciprocal \(P/Q\) in decreasing order.

For the uniform ball illustration, write \(C\) for the overlap of the two equal-volume balls, and \(t=P(C)=Q(C)\). The region exclusive to the first ball contributes up to \(1-t\) units of \(P\)-mass and zero \(Q\)-mass. If \(a>1-t\), the remaining \(a-1+t\) must come from the overlap, where the densities agree. The smallest possible \(Q(E)\) is \(\max\{0,a+t-1\}\). A binary guarantee based on this histogram requires that lower bound to exceed \(1/2\).

Gaussian likelihood ratios and the certified radius

Let \(P=N(x,\sigma^2I)\), \(Q=N(x+\delta,\sigma^2I)\) and \(L(z)=dQ/dP(z)\). Given fixed \(P\)-mass, a set of low likelihood ratio minimizes \(Q\)-mass. To prove it, choose \(H=\{L\le c\}\) with the desired mass, and compare any set \(E\) of equal mass:

\[Q(E)-Q(H)=\int (\mathbf1_E-\mathbf1_H)(L-c)\,dP\ge0.\]

Both products on the symmetric difference are nonnegative. Reversing the threshold proves the upper-bound version. For nonzero \(\delta\), the logarithm of \(L\) is \(\delta^\top(z-x)/\sigma^2-\|\delta\|^2/(2\sigma^2)\), monotone along \(\delta\). Consequently the extremal sets are halfspaces in that direction. Projection onto \(\delta/\|\delta\|\) shifts the mean by \(\|\delta\|\) and preserves standard deviation \(\sigma\). For any class \(A\) event with the stated lower bound, its \(Q\)-mass is at least

\[\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(\underline p_A)-\|\delta\|/\sigma),\]

and every other class has \(Q\)-mass at most \(\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(\overline p_B)+\|\delta\|/\sigma)\). Therefore the prediction is unchanged for

\[\|\delta\|<\frac{\sigma}{2}\big(\Phi^{-1}(\underline p_A)-\Phi^{-1}(\overline p_B)\big).\]

Comparing the two bounds is equivalent, by strict monotonicity of \(\Phi\), to the displayed strict radius inequality. When \(P(E_A)>\underline p_A\), the same threshold comparison has an extra term \(c(P(E_A)-\underline p_A)\ge0\). For an upper bound on a rival, take the upper tail of \(L\), reverse the signs, and use \(P(E)\le\overline p_B\). A class boundary of the base model inside the radius is compatible with the guarantee because the certified output is the maximum of the smoothed probabilities. If estimates come from sampling, use valid confidence bounds. Selecting \(A\) on an independent batch and taking a one-sided exact binomial lower bound on a fresh batch permits the conservative bound \(p_B\le1-p_A\). A larger \(\sigma\) increases the prefactor but may shrink the class-probability gap.

Challenge

For a binary halfspace base classifier with nonzero normal \(w\) and a point at positive signed geometric distance \(d\), derive its smoothed probability and show that the certified radius is exact.

Show solution

Gaussian projection along \(w/\|w\|\) has variance \(\sigma^2\), so the smoothed positive probability is \(\Phi(d/\sigma)\) and the negative probability is \(\Phi(-d/\sigma)\). The certificate radius is \(\sigma(\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(d/\sigma))-\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(-d/\sigma)))/2=d\). Smoothing leaves this halfspace’s decision boundary in place, because positive probability exceeds \(1/2\) precisely on its positive side. A shift along the negative normal of magnitude greater than \(d\) therefore switches the prediction.

Second challenge. Find the exact Gaussian smoothing certificate for a binary base classifier whose decision boundary is a hyperplane.

Show solution to the second challenge

Let \(h(z)=\mathbf1\{w^\top z\ge b\}\) and \(w\ne0\). With isotropic Gaussian noise, the positive class probability at \(x\) is \(p=\Phi((w^\top x-b)/(\sigma\|w\|))\). The runner-up has probability \(1-p\), so on the positive side the certificate is \(\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)=(w^\top x-b)/\|w\|\). Moving against the unit normal reaches the hyperplane at exactly this distance. The averaged classifier retains the original boundary.

Research challenge — sharpness of certification from class probabilities

Take \(0<p_B<p_A<1\) and \(p_A+p_B\le1\). Suppose there are enough classes to divide the leftover mass \(1-p_A-p_B\) into pieces at most \(p_B\). Prove Cohen et al.’s Theorem 2 in this setting. If

\[r>\frac{\sigma}{2}(\Phi^{-1}(p_A)-\Phi^{-1}(p_B)),\]

construct a deterministic base classifier with leading mass \(p_A\) and runner-up mass \(p_B\) at \(x\) whose smoothed class at \(x+ru\) differs from \(A\), for any unit direction \(u\). Explain what the inequality says about a particular trained model.

Show research challenge solution

Write \(t(z)=u^\top(z-x)/\sigma\). Assign class \(A\) to the half-space \(t(z)\le a\), with \(a=\Phi^{-1}(p_A)\), and class \(B\) to \(t(z)\ge b\), with \(b=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_B)=-\Phi^{-1}(p_B)\). Under the noise law at \(x\), \(t\) is standard normal. Since \(p_A\le1-p_B\), we have \(a\le b\) and the two half-spaces are disjoint up to a null boundary. Divide \((a,b)\) by normal quantiles into the remaining classes, assigning each a mass at most \(p_B\). The resulting classifier has the stated histogram and unique leading class \(A\) at \(x\).

At \(x+ru\), \(t\) is normal with mean \(r/\sigma\) and variance one. Class \(A\) has mass \(\Phi(a-r/\sigma)\) and class \(B\) has mass \(\Phi(r/\sigma-b)\). The second is strictly larger precisely when \(2r/\sigma>a+b\). The construction attains the likelihood-ratio lower and upper bounds simultaneously. Thus a certificate based only on this histogram cannot reach farther in general. A particular classifier can have a larger actual radius because its class regions may differ from these extremal half-spaces.
