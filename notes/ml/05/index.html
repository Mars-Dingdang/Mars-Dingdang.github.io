ML 05: PAC Learning and VC Dimension

Prove finite-class sample bounds and compute VC dimension by combining explicit realizations with impossible labelings.

Opposite vertices of the square share a label. The two convex hulls intersect at its center.

Realizable and agnostic PAC guarantees

In binary classification, \(R(h)\) is the probability of error on an independent test draw. Realizable PAC learning assumes some hypothesis in the class has zero risk. The learner must output a predictor with risk at most \(\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over the training sample. Agnostic PAC learning permits arbitrary labels and asks for risk within \(\varepsilon\) of \(\inf_{h\in\mathcal H}R(h)\).

For any fixed hypothesis of risk greater than \(\varepsilon\), the chance it makes no errors on \(n\) independent examples is \((1-R(h))^n\le e^{-n\varepsilon}\). A union bound over a finite class and the existence of a consistent hypothesis give the realizable ERM bound

\[n\ge\frac{\log|\mathcal H|+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}.\]

For the agnostic case, two-sided Hoeffding and a union bound give

\[P\!\left(\sup_{h\in\mathcal H}|\widehat R(h)-R(h)|>\varepsilon/2\right) \le 2|\mathcal H|e^{-n\varepsilon^2/2}.\]

On the complementary event, \(R(\widehat h)\le\widehat R(\widehat h)+\varepsilon/2\le\widehat R(h)+\varepsilon/2\le R(h)+\varepsilon\) for every \(h\). Take the infimum. A population-risk comparison with the Bayes predictor separates approximation error, \(\inf_{\mathcal H}R-R(h^\star)\), from the learner’s excess above the best in its class.

For a no-free-lunch example, select labels independently and uniformly for each point in a uniform domain of \(2n\) points, then draw \(n\) training examples. At least half the domain remains unseen. Conditional on the data, each unseen label remains an independent fair coin, giving expected risk at least \(1/4\) over the labeling and sample. Some deterministic labeling attains that lower bound in expectation over training draws. A random risk in \([0,1]\) satisfies \(\mathbb E R\le 1/8+(7/8)P(R\ge1/8)\), so for that labeling the learner has risk at least \(1/8\) with probability at least \(1/7\). If \(\mathcal H\) has infinite VC dimension, we can choose a shattered \(2n\)-point set for every \(n\). Its deterministic labeling is then realizable in \(\mathcal H\), so the bound contradicts any distribution-free PAC sample complexity at, for example, \(\varepsilon=1/9,\delta=1/8\).

Infinite classes can have small effective complexity

For thresholds \(h_t(x)=\mathbf1\{x\ge t\}\) on the line, two ordered points cannot receive \((1,0)\), but one point can receive either label. The VC dimension is 1. A single interval shatters two points, while three ordered points cannot receive \((1,0,1)\), so its dimension is 2.

For a realizable threshold at \(t^\star\), any consistent empirical minimizer has zero sample points in its error interval. If the empirical threshold is larger than \(t^\star\), the error interval is an initial segment of \([t^\star,\infty)\); if it is smaller, the error interval is a terminal segment of \((-\infty,t^\star)\). On each side with enough mass, choose the closest fixed segment of mass at least \(\varepsilon\), using the probability quantile and including the boundary atom if needed to reach \(\varepsilon\). Every error interval on that side of mass greater than \(\varepsilon\) contains this segment. Therefore the probability of any consistent threshold with risk greater than \(\varepsilon\) is at most \(2(1-\varepsilon)^n\). This directly yields order \(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\) samples. The same argument applies to concentric disks by replacing \(x\) with negative distance to the known center.

In \(\mathbb R^d\), an affine halfspace shatters \(d+1\) affinely independent points because an affine function interpolates arbitrary assigned values \(\pm1\). For any \(d+2\) points, the augmented vectors \((x_i,1)\) have a nontrivial linear dependence. Its coefficients sum to zero, and the positive and negative groups therefore have convex combinations representing the same point. Opposite labels on those groups cannot be realized. Thus the dimension is exactly \(d+1\).

Sauer–Shelah and the generalization bound

Let \(\Pi_d(n)\) denote the largest possible trace size on \(n\) points for VC dimension at most \(d\). Split the traces on the first \(n-1\) points into prefixes appearing at least once and prefixes that can be extended with either last label. The first family has VC dimension at most \(d\), and the second at most \(d-1\), because shattering \(d\) points with both extensions would shatter \(d+1\) points. Thus \(\Pi_d(n)\le\Pi_d(n-1)+\Pi_{d-1}(n-1)\). The base cases \(n=0\) and \(d=0\) have at most one trace. Pascal’s identity yields

\[\Pi_{\mathcal H}(n)\le\sum_{j=0}^d {n\choose j}\le(en/d)^d\qquad(1\le d\le n).\]

For the last inequality, multiply the binomial sum by \((d/n)^d\), use \((d/n)^d\le(d/n)^j\) for \(j\le d\), and bound \((1+d/n)^n\) by \(e^d\).

To see why this controls uniform convergence, add an independent ghost sample of size \(n\). For \(n\varepsilon^2\ge 2\), a hypothesis whose true and empirical risk differ by more than \(\varepsilon\) has at least a constant probability that its ghost error is within \(\varepsilon/2\) of the truth, by Chebyshev’s inequality for Bernoulli averages. The resulting two-sample event has a discrepancy above \(\varepsilon/2\). Swap the two members of each independent sample pair with a fair coin. Conditional on the pooled data, the discrepancy for any fixed trace is an average of independent signed terms bounded in magnitude by 1. Hoeffding and a union bound over at most \(\Pi_{\mathcal H}(2n)\) pooled prediction traces give

\[P\!\left(\sup_h|\widehat R(h)-R(h)|>\varepsilon\right) \le 4\Pi_{\mathcal H}(2n)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/8}\]

under the usual measurability assumptions. This gives an order \(\sqrt{(d\log(en/d)+\log(1/\delta))/n}\) bound for the empirical-population gap. The quantitative theorem uses more structure. In the typical parameter range \(0<\varepsilon\le1/16\), \(0<\delta\le1/8\), agnostic sample complexity is \(\Theta((d+\log(1/\delta))/\varepsilon^2)\) for \(d\ge1\). The course’s realizable bounds are \(\Omega((d+\log(1/\delta))/\varepsilon)\) and \(O((d\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta))/\varepsilon)\) for \(d\ge2\). The additional dimension restriction in the realizable lower-bound proof below supplies a second point where two concepts agree. The upper bound applies to consistent ERM. More elaborate learners remove the realizable logarithm. Hanneke, The Optimal Sample Complexity of PAC Learning, Theorem 2.

Why the realizable sample size scales as the inverse of the error

Fix a target \(h^\star\) and form the family of disagreement sets \(\mathcal A=\{\{x:h(x)\ne h^\star(x)\}:h\in\mathcal H\}\). On any finite set, forming a disagreement pattern flips exactly the coordinates on which \(h^\star\) is positive. This is a bijection of traces, preserving VC dimension. A consistent learner fails if some set of probability greater than \(\varepsilon\) receives no sample point. Such a failure is absent when the sample is an \(\varepsilon\)-net for \(\mathcal A\).

For a second independent sample of size \(n\), a fixed set of mass \(p>\varepsilon\) receives at least \(\varepsilon n/2\) ghost points with probability at least \(1/2\) for \(n\varepsilon\) larger than an absolute constant, by the multiplicative Chernoff lower tail. Conditional on the first sample and a witnessed bad set, the ghost sample is still independent, so the original failure probability is at most twice the probability of a set avoided by the first sample and hit at least \(\varepsilon n/2\) times by the ghost sample. Pool the \(2n\) points and make a uniform split into two groups of size \(n\). For a fixed trace with \(k\) hits on the pool, the probability all hits enter the ghost half is zero if \(k>n\) and otherwise is \((n)_k/(2n)_k\le2^{-k}\), where \((n)_k=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\). The contributing traces have \(k\ge\varepsilon n/2\), so

\[P(\text{a consistent }h\text{ has risk }>\varepsilon) \le2\Pi_{\mathcal H}(2n)2^{-\varepsilon n/2}.\]

Taking logarithms and applying Sauer–Shelah shows that \(n\ge C(d\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta))/\varepsilon\) suffices for an absolute \(C\). To check this substitution, set \(u=\log(1/\varepsilon)\) and \(v=\log(2/\delta)/d\). At \(n=Cd(u+v)/\varepsilon\), the positive exponent per dimension is at most \(u+\log(2eC)+\log(u+v)+v\), while the negative exponent per dimension is \(C(u+v)\log2/2\). For large enough \(C\), the latter dominates.

For a dimension lower bound, choose a shattered set of \(d\ge2\) points, putting mass \(p=8\varepsilon\) uniformly on \(d-1\) rare points and the remaining mass on an anchor with a fixed label. Independently randomize the target labels on the rare points. If \(n\le(d-1)/(2p)\), the expected number of unseen rare points is at least \((d-1)/2\) because \((1-p/(d-1))^n\ge1-np/(d-1)\). Every unseen target bit is a fair coin conditional on the data, so the expected risk on just the rare points is at least \(p/4\). This risk stays in \([0,p]\), giving probability at least \((p/4-\varepsilon)/(p-\varepsilon)=1/7\) that it exceeds \(\varepsilon\). Averaging guarantees such a deterministic target, so \(n=\Omega(d/\varepsilon)\) is necessary at constant confidence.

For confidence, instead put mass \(2\varepsilon\) on one rare point and the rest on the anchor. Take two targets differing only on the rare point. When no rare point is observed, the data have identical distributions, and the average conditional probability of predicting its label incorrectly is \(1/2\). Therefore some target gives error exceeding \(\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(\tfrac12(1-2\varepsilon)^n\). Since \(-\log(1-2\varepsilon)\le4\varepsilon\) for \(\varepsilon\le1/4\), this forces \(n=\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon)\). The maximum of the two lower bounds is within a factor two of their sum.

Why the agnostic dimension term needs no logarithm

The single union bound used above pays for all traces even when two traces differ on very few points. A sequence of increasingly fine covers controls these differences at their own scales. For a fixed sample, put the normalized Euclidean metric \(\|a\|_n^2=n^{-1}\sum_i a_i^2\) on its finite set of binary loss vectors.

A set of \(M\ge2\) loss vectors separated by distance more than \(r\) has normalized Hamming distance more than \(r^2\) between each pair. Pick \(s=\lceil 3\log M/r^2\rceil\) sample coordinates independently and uniformly with replacement. Any pair agrees on every chosen coordinate with probability at most \(e^{-sr^2}\). The union bound over pairs is less than one, so some coordinate sample distinguishes all \(M\). Add more coordinates if needed to ensure \(s\ge d\). Sauer–Shelah then implies

\[\log M\le d\log\!\left(C\left(1+\frac{\log M}{d r^2}\right)\right).\]

To solve this, write \(Y=\max(1,\log M/d)\). Since \(r\le1\), the right side after division by \(d\) is at most \(\log(C'/r^2)+\log Y\). The inequality \(\log Y\le Y/2\) for \(Y\ge1\) gives \(\log M=O(d\log(C''/r))\). A maximal separated set covers the loss vectors within radius \(r\), so the same logarithmic bound holds for covering numbers.

Let \(\sigma_i\) be independent fair signs. For any finite family of vectors \(A\) with normalized norms at most \(s\), the signed average of a fixed vector is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy \(s^2/n\): its exponential moment is the product of \(\cosh(t a_i/n)\le e^{t^2 a_i^2/(2n^2)}\). Bounding the exponential moment of the maximum by summing over the family and optimizing \(t\) gives \(\mathbb E_\sigma\max_{a\in A}|n^{-1}\sum_i\sigma_i a_i|\le s\sqrt{2\log(2|A|)/n}\).

Take a fixed reference vector at scale 1 and a cover at every radius \(2^{-j}\). Approximate each vector by a nearest cover element at each scale. Consecutive approximations differ in norm by at most \(2^{-j}+2^{-j+1}=3\cdot2^{-j}\). There are at most the products of the consecutive cover sizes possible differences. Apply the signed maximum bound to the allowable differences at each scale and sum. The remainder vanishes as the radius tends to zero because the signed average of a residual of norm \(r\) has magnitude at most \(r\) by Cauchy–Schwarz. The reference contributes at most \(1/\sqrt n\) in mean absolute value and the increments give

\[\mathbb E_\sigma\sup_h\left|n^{-1}\sum_i\sigma_i\mathbf1\{h(x_i)\ne y_i\}\right| \le \frac1{\sqrt n}+C\sqrt{\frac dn}\sum_{j\ge1}2^{-j}\sqrt{j+1} =O(\sqrt{d/n}).\]

To pass to risks, move the ghost expectation outside the supremum by Jensen, swap the sample pairs with fair signs, and apply the triangle inequality to separate the two signed sums. This bounds the expected uniform gap by twice the signed complexity above. Changing one sample point changes the uniform gap by at most \(1/n\), so bounded differences adds \(\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(2n)}\) with confidence \(1-\delta\). Comparing two risks through their empirical versions costs at most twice the uniform gap. This gives the stated agnostic upper bound, using chaining in place of the single-scale argument.

Agnostic lower bounds from noisy labels

For the dimension term, choose \(d\) shattered points uniformly. Index distributions by \(\theta\in\{-1,+1\}^d\) and let \(P_\theta(Y=1\mid X=x_i)=(1+b\theta_i)/2\) for \(0<b\le1/2\). The Bayes rule belongs to the restricted class. A predictor disagreeing with the signs on \(k\) points has excess risk \(bk/d\). Flipping one bit of \(\theta\) changes the one-observation KL divergence by

\[\frac b d\log\frac{1+b}{1-b}\le \frac{4b^2}{d}.\]

For \(n\) observations KL multiplies by \(n\). By Pinsker, if \(n\le d/(32b^2)\) the total variation between those whole-sample laws is at most \(1/4\). Under equal prior weights, the average probability of guessing their differing bit incorrectly is at least \((1-\mathrm{TV})/2\ge3/8\): for any decision event the sum of the two errors is at least \(1-\mathrm{TV}\). Pair the uniform prior on the hypercube along each coordinate and sum the resulting error probabilities. The expected excess is at least \(3b/8\), while it is at most \(b\) on each trial. For \(b=8\varepsilon\), the probability of excess greater than \(\varepsilon=b/8\) is at least \(2/7\). Some deterministic sign vector attains this failure probability.

For the confidence term use a single shattered point with competing label laws \(p_\pm=(1\pm b)/2\) and \(b=2\varepsilon\). For two laws with densities \(p,q\) and affinity \(S=\int\sqrt{pq}\), Cauchy–Schwarz applied to \(\int|\sqrt p-\sqrt q|(\sqrt p+\sqrt q)\) yields \(\mathrm{TV}\le\sqrt{1-S^2}\). Thus any binary test has average error at least \((1-\sqrt{1-S^2})/2\ge S^2/4\). The affinity of these Bernoulli distributions is \(\sqrt{1-b^2}\) and affinities multiply over independent observations, so the failure probability of at least one distribution is at least \((1-b^2)^n/4\). As \(-\log(1-b^2)\le2b^2\) for \(b\le1/2\), achieving confidence \(1-\delta\) requires \(n=\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^2)\). Combining with the dimension argument proves the agnostic lower bound against arbitrary learning rules.

Challenges

Problem 1

Find the VC dimension of all subsets of the real line that are unions of at most \(k\) intervals. Permit the empty set.

Show solution

The dimension is \(2k\). Among \(2k\) ordered points, any binary labeling has at most \(k\) contiguous runs of positives. Put one interval around each positive run, excluding neighboring negative points. For \(2k+1\) ordered points the alternating labeling starting and ending with 1 has \(k+1\) positive runs. Every interval joining any two of those runs contains a negative point. That labeling is impossible on every set of \(2k+1\) points.

Problem 2

Compare the VC dimensions of \(\mathbf1\{a\sin x>0\}\), \(\mathbf1\{\sin(x+a)>0\}\), and \(\mathbf1\{\sin(ax)>0\}\).

Show solution

The amplitude class has three distinct functions and can shatter one point, giving dimension 1. Phase shifts produce open semicircles of positives. The points \(0,\pi/2\) admit all four labels. Two equal residues modulo \(2\pi\) cannot receive different labels. Among three distinct residues, either they cannot all fit in an open semicircle, or they can be ordered within one. In the latter case any open semicircle containing the two extremes contains the short arc between them, so the middle point cannot be negative. The dimension is 2.

For frequency scaling use \(x_i=2^{i-1}\) for any \(n\). Given labels \(y_i\), put \(b_i=1-y_i\) and take \(a=2\pi(\sum_{j=1}^n b_j2^{-j}+2^{-n-1})\). The fractional part of \(ax_i/(2\pi)\) is \(b_i/2\) plus a number strictly between 0 and \(1/2\). Its sine is positive precisely when \(b_i=0\). Hence these \(n\) points are shattered for arbitrary \(n\).

Problem 3. Generalization from a sample compression scheme

Suppose a realizable learner keeps at most \(k\) labeled observations and a side message from a fixed alphabet of size \(B\). A deterministic reconstruction function takes the retained observations in input order and the side message, and its prediction is consistent on the full sample. Prove a sample compression bound. Apply it to closed axis-aligned rectangles in \(\mathbb R^d\) by keeping examples that witness the extremes. Floyd and Warmuth, Sample Compression, Learnability, and the Vapnik–Chervonenkis Dimension, §2.

Show solution

Fix a subset of sample indices \(I\) and a side message. Condition on the observations at those indices. The reconstructed classifier is now fixed, while all remaining points are independent draws. If its risk exceeds \(\varepsilon\), its chance of matching all omitted labels is at most \((1-\varepsilon)^{n-|I|}\). This applies even though the actual compressor can choose its subset and message after seeing the omitted data, because we union bound over all fixed choices. The probability of a consistent reconstructed rule of risk above \(\varepsilon\) is at most

\[B\sum_{j=0}^{k}{n\choose j}(1-\varepsilon)^{n-j} \le B(en/k)^k e^{-\varepsilon(n-k)}\qquad(1\le k\le n).\]

For a sample with positives, retain one positive point minimizing and one maximizing each coordinate, at most \(2d\) points. Reconstruction takes their coordinatewise bounding box. It contains every positive sample point and is contained in the target rectangle, so contains no negative sample point. When the retained set is empty, reconstruct the all-negative predictor. This gives compression with \(k=2d\) and \(B=1\). In the plane the four extreme witnesses also explain the VC upper bound: on any five points, mark four points witnessing the extrema positive and an unneeded point negative, which no rectangle can realize. The four cardinal points of a diamond are shattered by bounding the chosen positive points, so the planar VC dimension is exactly four.
