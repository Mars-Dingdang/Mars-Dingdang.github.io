ML 08: Decision Trees, Random Forests, and Boosting

Compute a greedy tree split, derive AdaBoost’s training and margin bounds, and connect gradient boosting to functional descent.

The weighted post-split impurity is about 0.317, below the parent impurity of 0.480.

Greedy splits and bagging

For class proportions \(p_c\), Gini impurity is \(I(p)=1-\sum_c p_c^2\), the chance of different labels in two independent draws. A split maximizes parent impurity minus the sample-count weighted impurity of its children. The illustrated root has 6 blue and 4 orange examples. Its left child has 5 blue and 1 orange, giving \(5/18\), and its right child has 1 blue and 3 orange, giving \(3/8\). The weighted child value is \((6/10)(5/18)+(4/10)(3/8)=19/60\). Each node optimizes one split. Depth limits, minimum leaf counts, and pruning control the eventual tree size. Replacing Gini by entropy gives information gain.

A bootstrap sample draws \(n\) times with replacement from \(n\) cases. A specific case is absent with probability \((1-1/n)^n\to e^{-1}\), so the trees that leave it out can supply its out-of-bag prediction. A random forest also samples feature candidates at each split. For conditional prediction variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\) in an ensemble model, expanding the variance of the mean over \(B\) trees gives \(\sigma^2(\rho+(1-\rho)/B)\). The correlation term survives as the number of trees increases.

For the ten observations on slides 6–9, the root’s up/down counts are \((4,6)\) and its Gini impurity is \(12/25\). The three candidates have the following root scores:

Split Child up/down counts Weighted Gini
Past trend \((4,2)\) and \((0,4)\) \(4/15\)
Open interest \((2,2)\) and \((2,4)\) \(7/15\)
Trading volume \((4,3)\) and \((0,3)\) \(12/35\)

Past trend therefore gives the lowest weighted impurity, equivalently the greatest gain. Its negative branch is pure. On the positive branch, open interest gives \((2,0)\) and \((2,2)\), with weighted impurity \(1/3\). Volume gives \((4,0)\) and \((0,2)\), both pure. The finished tree predicts up exactly when trend is positive and volume high. Splitting on a continuous feature works similarly after sorting its distinct values and trying thresholds between successive values. For \(C\) classes, Gini is maximized at the uniform distribution with value \(1-1/C\), by Cauchy–Schwarz.

AdaBoost from exponential loss

For labels and weak predictions in \(\{-1,1\}\), write \(F_t=F_{t-1}+\alpha_t h_t\). At round \(t\), set \(D_t(i)\propto e^{-y_iF_{t-1}(x_i)}\) and let \(\epsilon_t\) be the error of \(h_t\) under \(D_t\). For fixed \(h_t\), adding the new learner multiplies the empirical exponential loss by

\[Z_t(\alpha)=(1-\epsilon_t)e^{-\alpha}+\epsilon_t e^\alpha.\]

Differentiation gives the minimum at \(\alpha_t=\tfrac12\log((1-\epsilon_t)/\epsilon_t)\) when \(0<\epsilon_t<1/2\). Substituting gives \(Z_t=2\sqrt{\epsilon_t(1-\epsilon_t)}\). The new sample weight is \(D_{t+1}(i)=D_t(i)e^{-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)}/Z_t\). A perfect weak rule can terminate boosting, and error \(1/2\) gives zero coefficient.

With \(F_0=0\), unrolling the weight recurrence gives \(D_{T+1}(i)=e^{-y_iF_T(x_i)}/(n\prod_t Z_t)\), since the initial weight is \(1/n\). Summing over \(i\) gives the empirical exponential loss \(\prod_t Z_t\). Misclassification implies \(y_iF_T(x_i)\le0\), and then its exponential term is at least 1. Writing \(\epsilon_t=1/2-\gamma_t\) gives

\[\widehat R(\operatorname{sign}F_T)\le\prod_t\sqrt{1-4\gamma_t^2}\le e^{-2\sum_t\gamma_t^2}.\]

Moreover, for the normalized margin \(m_i=y_iF_T(x_i)/\sum_t\alpha_t\), a threshold \(\theta\) gives

\[\widehat P(m_i\le\theta) \le e^{\theta\sum_t\alpha_t}\prod_t Z_t =\prod_t 2\,\epsilon_t^{(1-\theta)/2}(1-\epsilon_t)^{(1+\theta)/2}.\]

The first inequality compares an indicator at threshold \(\theta\) with \(\exp(\theta\sum_t\alpha_t-y_iF_T(x_i))\). Suppose \(\epsilon_t\le1/2-\gamma\) for all \(t\), with \(0<\gamma<1/2\). The factor on the right is increasing in \(\epsilon_t\) for \(\epsilon_t<(1-\theta)/2\), as differentiating its logarithm shows. For \(0\le\theta<\gamma\), each factor is thus bounded by \((1-2\gamma)^{(1-\theta)/2}(1+2\gamma)^{(1+\theta)/2}<1\). To see the strict inequality, set \(u=2\gamma\). The log of this upper bound is \(\tfrac12\log(1-u^2)+\theta\operatorname{atanh}(u)\). The inequality \(-\log(1-u^2)\ge u\operatorname{atanh}(u)\) follows since their difference has derivative \(u/(1-u^2)-\operatorname{atanh}(u)\ge0\) and vanishes at zero. Since \(\theta<u/2\), the log is negative. This gives the exponential decay in slides 33–35. For the zero-margin bound, \(\sum_t\gamma_t^2=\infty\) suffices for convergence to zero, in particular when every edge is at least \(\gamma>0\).

The weight update is also mirror descent on the sample simplex, as asked on slide 19. Fix \(h_t\) and put \(g_i=y_i h_t(x_i)\). The update minimizes \(\alpha_t g^\top D+D_{\rm KL}(D\|D_t)\). Its interior stationarity condition is \(\alpha_t g_i+\log(D_i/D_t(i))+1+\nu=0\), where \(\nu\) enforces \(\sum_iD_i=1\). Exponentiating and normalizing gives the AdaBoost weights. Thus correctly classified examples lose relative weight and mistakes gain it.

On slides 20–24, the first of three stumps misses 3 of 10 samples, giving \(\epsilon_1=3/10\) and \(\alpha_1=\log(7/3)/2\). Under \(D_2\), those three each weigh \(1/6\) and the remaining seven each weigh \(1/14\). The second stump misses three of the latter, so \(\epsilon_2=3/14\) and \(\alpha_2=\log(11/3)/2\). The update divides correct weights by \(2(1-\epsilon_2)=11/7\) and incorrect weights by \(2\epsilon_2=3/7\). The third stump misses three samples of weight \(1/22\) each, giving \(\epsilon_3=3/22\) and \(\alpha_3=\log(19/3)/2\). Since the three positive coefficients satisfy the strict triangle inequalities, every pair’s total exceeds the third coefficient. Their final weighted vote therefore agrees with the majority of the three stumps in this example.

Functional gradient boosting

Treat the predictions \(F(x_i)\) as coordinates. With squared loss, the negative gradient is the observed residual \(y_i-F(x_i)\). With a general differentiable loss, set

\[r_i=-\left.\frac{\partial\ell(y_i,u)}{\partial u}\right|_{u=F(x_i)}.\]

Fit a weak regressor to \((x_i,r_i)\), line-search its coefficient, and update the predictions. A tree fits an allowable approximation to the descent direction. For squared loss and a fixed tree partition, minimizing \(\sum_{i\in A}(r_i-c_A)^2/2\) on a leaf gives \(c_A=\sum_{i\in A}r_i/|A|\) by differentiation. The normal equations for that orthogonal projection also give \(r^\top h=\|h\|_2^2\). Hence shrinking the tree by \(0<\eta\le1\) decreases the squared loss by \(\eta(1-\eta/2)\|h\|_2^2\) unless \(h=0\). The lecture’s sample predictions \((0.8,1.4)\) and targets \((0.9,1.3)\) give residual targets \((0.1,-0.1)\) for the next tree.

A uniform margin bound, with constants

Fix a margin threshold \(0<\theta\le1\). For a normalized vote \(f\), draw \(N\) independent base hypotheses from its weights, and write \(g\) for their average. When \(yf(x)\le0\), Hoeffding gives \(\Pr_g(yg(x)>\theta/2)\le a=e^{-N\theta^2/8}\). When \(yf(x)>\theta\), it gives \(\Pr_g(yg(x)\le\theta/2)\le a\). Therefore

\[P(yf\le0)\le \mathbb E_g P(yg\le\theta/2)+a,\qquad \mathbb E_g P_S(yg\le\theta/2)\le P_S(yf\le\theta)+a.\]

For every one of the at most \(|\mathcal H|^N\) possible ordered sampled votes, apply the one-sided Hoeffding bound to its binary thresholded loss over \(n\) independent training examples. A union bound gives, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), \(P(yg\le\theta/2)\le P_S(yg\le\theta/2)+b\) simultaneously, where \(b=\sqrt{(N\log|\mathcal H|+\log(1/\delta))/(2n)}\). The event does not depend on the vote weights. Combining the two displays yields the uniform gap \(2a+b\). For \(n\ge2\), choose \(N=\lceil4\log n/\theta^2\rceil\) so \(a\le1/\sqrt n\).

Slide 32 states the result simultaneously for all margins. To recover that quantifier, first apply the bound at \(\theta_j=2^{-j}\) for \(j\ge0\), giving the \(j\)th event failure budget \(\delta/((j+1)(j+2))\). These budgets sum to \(\delta\). For an arbitrary \(0<\theta\le1\), choose \(\theta_j\le\theta<2\theta_j\). Then \(P_S(yf\le\theta_j)\le P_S(yf\le\theta)\), and \(\theta_j^{-2}\le4\theta^{-2}\). For \(\theta\ge n^{-1/2}\) the extra confidence cost is only \(O(\log(1+\log n))\), absorbed by the leading term when \(|\mathcal H|\ge2\). For smaller \(\theta\) the displayed big-\(O\) bound is already at least one with a universal constant. The single-base-rule case is ordinary Hoeffding. This proves the margin theorem with order \(\sqrt{(\log|\mathcal H|\log n/\theta^2+\log(1/\delta))/n}\). Schapire et al., “Boosting the Margin,” Theorem 1

Challenge

Show that for finite base class, the first AdaBoost round is a greedy coordinate minimization of exponential loss, and give the exact objective improvement when the selected rule has weighted error \(\epsilon\). What changes if the class contains each sign-flipped rule?

Show solution

For coordinate \(h\), the minimized multiplicative ratio is \(2\sqrt{\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}\) when the new coefficient is unconstrained. If coefficients may only increase, its optimum is at zero whenever \(\epsilon\ge1/2\). On \([0,1/2]\) the ratio increases with \(\epsilon\), so the best permitted coordinate is the lowest-error rule. The decrease is current loss times \(1-2\sqrt{\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}\). With a closed-under-sign-flip class, a negative coefficient on \(h\) has the same effect as a positive coefficient on \(-h\), whose weighted error is \(1-\epsilon\).

Second challenge. Derive the optimal second-order score for a new tree split when leaf weights have an \(\ell_2\) penalty.

Show solution to the second challenge

For a leaf let \(G\) and \(H\) be the sums of sample gradients and Hessians at the current predictions. Its quadratic term is \(Gw+\tfrac12(H+\lambda)w^2\). If \(H+\lambda>0\), the minimizer is \(-G/(H+\lambda)\) and the fitted contribution is \(-G^2/(2(H+\lambda))\). Thus split gain is half the sum of the two new \(G^2/(H+\lambda)\) scores minus half the old score and minus the extra leaf penalty. A large Newton weight alone gives no guarantee of a large gain.

Paper challenge · When a Newton split improves the tree

In the XGBoost quadratic fit, let the two child nodes have gradient sums \(G_L,G_R\) and positive Hessian sums \(H_L,H_R\). First set the leaf ridge penalty \(\lambda=0\) and prove that the unpenalized gain before the extra leaf cost is a weighted square of the difference of the two Newton steps. Then construct a split with \(\lambda>0\) that has strictly negative gain even when the extra leaf cost vanishes. Chen and Guestrin, “XGBoost,” §2.2, equations (5)–(7)

Show paper solution

Put \(a=G_L/H_L\) and \(b=G_R/H_R\). These ratios are the negatives of the separate Newton steps. Twice the score improvement at \(\lambda=0\) equals

\[H_L a^2+H_R b^2-\frac{(H_La+H_Rb)^2}{H_L+H_R} =\frac{H_LH_R}{H_L+H_R}(a-b)^2.\]

This is nonnegative, with equality exactly when one weight fits both child Newton steps. For \(\lambda>0\), choose identically sized children with \(G_L=G_R=1\) and \(H_L=H_R=1\). Twice the gain is \(2/(1+\lambda)-4/(2+\lambda)\), which is strictly negative. Duplicating a nonzero fitted parent weight on its children incurs the ridge penalty twice, so the two-leaf model has a different regularized objective even before the split cost is included.
