ML 02: Gradient Descent and Convergence

Derive gradient descent from the smoothness bound and prove its rates for convex, strongly convex, and nonconvex objectives.

The larger stable step oscillates along the high-curvature axis. The small step follows a smoother path.

Smoothness gives a quadratic bound

On an unconstrained Euclidean space the step is \(x^+=x-\eta\nabla f(x)\). It minimizes the quadratic local model \(\langle\nabla f(x),d\rangle+\|d\|^2/(2\eta)\) over the displacement \(d\). For an \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient, integrate along \(d=y-x\):

\[\begin{aligned} f(y)-f(x)-\langle \nabla f(x),d\rangle &=\int_0^1\langle\nabla f(x+td)-\nabla f(x),d\rangle\,dt,\\ |f(y)-f(x)-\langle \nabla f(x),d\rangle|&\le L\|d\|^2/2. \end{aligned}\]

For twice continuously differentiable \(f\) this two-sided remainder bound implies \(|u^\top\nabla^2 f(x)u|\le L\) for each unit \(u\) by substituting \(d=hu\), dividing by \(h^2/2\), and taking \(h\to0\). The Hessian is symmetric, so its operator norm is bounded by \(L\). Integrating the Hessian along the segment then gives the original Lipschitz condition. Analogously the lower remainder bound \(\mu\|d\|^2/2\) is equivalent to \(\nabla^2f\succeq\mu I\). In one direction, take the directional limit. In the other, apply convexity to \(f(x)-\mu\|x\|^2/2\) using its positive semidefinite Hessian. Convexity itself is the \(\mu=0\) case.

Two other forms follow from the same argument. Apply Jensen’s inequality to \(f-\mu\|\cdot\|^2/2\) and expand the square:

\[f((1-t)x+ty)\le(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)-\tfrac\mu2 t(1-t)\|x-y\|^2.\]

Conversely, subtracting the quadratic converts this inequality into ordinary convexity. For differentiable \(f\), add the lower remainder bounds with \(x,y\) exchanged to obtain strong gradient monotonicity,

\[\langle\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\ge\mu\|y-x\|^2.\]

Conversely, apply that inequality to \(x\) and \(x+td\). For \(t>0\) divide by \(t\) to give \(\langle\nabla f(x+td)-\nabla f(x),d\rangle\ge\mu t\|d\|^2\). Integrating from zero to one gives the lower remainder bound.

With \(d=-\eta g\), \(g=\nabla f(x)\), smoothness yields

\[f(x^+)\le f(x)-\eta(1-L\eta/2)\|g\|^2.\]

For \(0<\eta\le1/L\), the decrease is at least \(\eta\|g\|^2/2\).

Convex objectives

Convexity gives \(f(x)-f^\star\le\langle g,x-x^\star\rangle\) for any minimizer \(x^\star\). Combine this with the decrease and expand the next squared distance:

\[f(x_{t+1})-f^\star\le \langle g_t,x_t-x^\star\rangle-\tfrac\eta2\|g_t\|^2 =\frac{\|x_t-x^\star\|^2-\|x_{t+1}-x^\star\|^2}{2\eta}.\]

Sum from \(t=0\) to \(T-1\). The distances telescope. Since the losses are nonincreasing, the last loss is at most their average, giving \(f(x_T)-f^\star\le\|x_0-x^\star\|^2/(2\eta T)\).

Strong convexity

The \(\mu\)-strongly convex lower quadratic model has minimum \(f(x)-\|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2\mu)\). Evaluating the lower bound at a minimizer shows \(f(x)-f^\star\le\|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2\mu)\). Plugging this into descent with \(\eta=1/L\) yields

\[f(x_T)-f^\star\le (1-\mu/L)^T[f(x_0)-f^\star].\]

For a quadratic with Hessian \(A\), the \(i\)th eigencoordinate is multiplied by \(1-\eta\lambda_i\) each step. A large ratio \(L/\mu\) means a step chosen for the steep direction can move slowly along the shallow direction.

Nonconvex objectives

If \(f\) is smooth and bounded below, summing the decrease yields

\[\min_{0\le t<T}\|\nabla f(x_t)\|^2\le \frac{2(f(x_0)-\inf f)}{\eta T}.\]

This controls a first-order stationary point. For example, \(x^2-y^2+y^4\) is bounded below and has a stationary point at the origin, where the Hessian has one negative eigenvalue. A twice differentiable local minimizer has zero gradient and a positive semidefinite Hessian by restriction to lines. If its Hessian is positive definite, Taylor’s theorem gives a strict local minimum.

Full gradients for \(f=n^{-1}\sum_i f_i\) cost \(n\) component gradients. Uniform subsampling gives a cheaper unbiased direction whose variance and convergence properties are addressed in Lecture 3.

Challenges

Problem 1

For positive-definite quadratic \(f(x)=\tfrac12(x-x^\star)^\top A(x-x^\star)\) with eigenvalues in \([\mu,L]\), find the fixed step minimizing the worst-case one-step contraction in the \(A\)-norm.

Show solution

The squared \(A\)-norm is a weighted sum of squared eigencoordinates. Its norm contraction is bounded by \(\max_{\lambda\in[\mu,L]}|1-\eta\lambda|=\max\{|1-\eta\mu|,|1-\eta L|\}\). For \(\eta\in[1/L,1/\mu]\) the endpoints have opposite signs, so equality of their magnitudes gives \(\eta=2/(L+\mu)\). On either side of that balancing point the larger magnitude increases. The factor is \((L-\mu)/(L+\mu)\), and the function gap contracts by its square. The factor is attained by an extreme eigenvector if the endpoints are eigenvalues.

Problem 2

Suppose \(2\mu(f(x)-f^\star)\le\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\) and \(f\) is \(L\)-smooth. Prove a linear objective-gap rate and give a convex example satisfying the assumption without being strongly convex.

Show solution

The \(1/L\) step decreases \(f\) by at least \(\|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2L)\ge(\mu/L)(f(x)-f^\star)\). Iterate this inequality to get the factor \((1-\mu/L)^T\). The example \(f(x,y)=x^2/2\) gives equality with \(\mu=1\), while its line of minimizers precludes strong convexity.
