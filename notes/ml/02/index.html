ML 02: Gradient Descent and Convergence

Derive gradient descent from the smoothness bound and prove its rates for convex, strongly convex, and nonconvex objectives.

The larger stable step oscillates along the high-curvature axis. The small step follows a smoother path.

Function values, derivatives, and stationary points

A zeroth-order oracle returns \(f(x)\). A first-order oracle also returns the gradient, and a second-order oracle provides Hessian information. A dense \(d\)-parameter Hessian has \(d^2\) entries, motivating methods that work with Hessian-vector products or update curvature approximations.

The \(K\)-Lipschitz condition on function values is \(|f(x)-f(y)|\le K\|x-y\|\), which is distinct from an \(L\)-Lipschitz condition on gradients. For differentiable functions the former is equivalent to \(\|\nabla f(x)\|\le K\). A bounded gradient implies the value bound by integrating along a line. Conversely, apply the value bound to \(x+tu\) for a unit direction \(u\) and divide by \(|t|\) before taking \(t\to0\), then maximize over \(u\). For example, \(x^2\) is gradient-Lipschitz on the line and its values are not globally Lipschitz.

The lecture’s cubic \(f(x)=x^3-3x+2\) has stationary points at \(x=\pm1\). Since \(f''(-1)=-6\) and \(f''(1)=6\), one is a strict local maximum and the other is a strict local minimum. Their shared zero derivative illustrates why stationarity alone gives only a necessary condition for unconstrained local minimization.

Smoothness gives a quadratic bound

On an unconstrained Euclidean space the step is \(x^+=x-\eta\nabla f(x)\). It minimizes the quadratic local model \(\langle\nabla f(x),d\rangle+\|d\|^2/(2\eta)\) over the displacement \(d\). For an \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient, integrate along \(d=y-x\):

\[\begin{aligned} f(y)-f(x)-\langle \nabla f(x),d\rangle &=\int_0^1\langle\nabla f(x+td)-\nabla f(x),d\rangle\,dt,\\ |f(y)-f(x)-\langle \nabla f(x),d\rangle|&\le L\|d\|^2/2. \end{aligned}\]

For twice continuously differentiable \(f\) this two-sided remainder bound implies \(|u^\top\nabla^2 f(x)u|\le L\) for each unit \(u\) by substituting \(d=hu\), dividing by \(h^2/2\), and taking \(h\to0\). The Hessian is symmetric, so its operator norm is bounded by \(L\). Integrating the Hessian along the segment then gives the original Lipschitz condition. Analogously the lower remainder bound \(\mu\|d\|^2/2\) is equivalent to \(\nabla^2f\succeq\mu I\). In one direction, take the directional limit. In the other, apply convexity to \(f(x)-\mu\|x\|^2/2\) using its positive semidefinite Hessian. Convexity itself is the \(\mu=0\) case.

Two other forms follow from the same argument. Apply Jensen’s inequality to \(f-\mu\|\cdot\|^2/2\) and expand the square:

\[f((1-t)x+ty)\le(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)-\tfrac\mu2 t(1-t)\|x-y\|^2.\]

Conversely, subtracting the quadratic converts this inequality into ordinary convexity. For differentiable \(f\), add the lower remainder bounds with \(x,y\) exchanged to obtain strong gradient monotonicity,

\[\langle\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\ge\mu\|y-x\|^2.\]

Conversely, apply that inequality to \(x\) and \(x+td\). For \(t>0\) divide by \(t\) to give \(\langle\nabla f(x+td)-\nabla f(x),d\rangle\ge\mu t\|d\|^2\). Integrating from zero to one gives the lower remainder bound.

With \(d=-\eta g\), \(g=\nabla f(x)\), smoothness yields

\[f(x^+)\le f(x)-\eta(1-L\eta/2)\|g\|^2.\]

For \(0<\eta\le1/L\), the decrease is at least \(\eta\|g\|^2/2\).

Convex objectives

Convexity gives \(f(x)-f^\star\le\langle g,x-x^\star\rangle\) for any minimizer \(x^\star\). Combine this with the decrease and expand the next squared distance:

\[f(x_{t+1})-f^\star\le \langle g_t,x_t-x^\star\rangle-\tfrac\eta2\|g_t\|^2 =\frac{\|x_t-x^\star\|^2-\|x_{t+1}-x^\star\|^2}{2\eta}.\]

Sum from \(t=0\) to \(T-1\). The distances telescope. Since the losses are nonincreasing, the last loss is at most their average, giving \(f(x_T)-f^\star\le\|x_0-x^\star\|^2/(2\eta T)\).

Strong convexity

The \(\mu\)-strongly convex lower quadratic model has minimum \(f(x)-\|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2\mu)\). Evaluating the lower bound at a minimizer shows \(f(x)-f^\star\le\|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2\mu)\). Plugging this into descent with \(\eta=1/L\) yields

\[f(x_T)-f^\star\le (1-\mu/L)^T[f(x_0)-f^\star].\]

For a quadratic with Hessian \(A\), the \(i\)th eigencoordinate is multiplied by \(1-\eta\lambda_i\) each step. A large ratio \(L/\mu\) means a step chosen for the steep direction can move slowly along the shallow direction.

Distance contraction and the full stable step interval

For convex \(L\)-smooth \(f\) on \(\mathbb R^d\), define \(g_z(u)=f(u)-\langle\nabla f(z),u\rangle\). It is minimized at \(z\), so one \(1/L\) gradient step at \(x\) for \(g_z\) gives

\[f(x)-f(z)-\langle\nabla f(z),x-z\rangle \ge\frac1{2L}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(z)\|^2.\]

Add the inequality with \(x,z\) exchanged to get \(\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(z),x-z\rangle\ge\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(z)\|^2/L\). Expanding the distance to \(x^\star\) under the update and applying this bound at \(z=x^\star\) gives

\[\|x_{t+1}-x^\star\|^2\le\|x_t-x^\star\|^2-\eta(2/L-\eta)\|\nabla f(x_t)\|^2.\]

So the distances never increase for \(0<\eta<2/L\). With \(R=\|x_0-x^\star\|\), convexity bounds \(\Delta_t=f(x_t)-f^\star\le R\|\nabla f(x_t)\|\). The descent lemma therefore gives \(\Delta_{t+1}\le\Delta_t-\eta(1-L\eta/2)\Delta_t^2/R^2\). Unless an iterate already attains the optimum, subtract reciprocal gaps to find

\[\Delta_T\le\left[\Delta_0^{-1}+T\eta(1-L\eta/2)/R^2\right]^{-1}.\]

This proves the supplement’s convex rate for the full step interval. If \(f\) is also \(\mu\)-strongly convex with \(L>\mu\), apply the gradient inner-product bound to \(f(x)-\mu\|x\|^2/2\), convex and \((L-\mu)\)-smooth. Expanding and rearranging gives, for \(d=x-z\) and \(q=\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(z)\),

\[\langle q,d\rangle\ge\frac{\mu L}{\mu+L}\|d\|^2+\frac1{\mu+L}\|q\|^2.\]

Substitute \(z=x^\star\) in the distance expansion to get \(\|x_{t+1}-x^\star\|^2\le[1-2\eta\mu L/(\mu+L)]\|x_t-x^\star\|^2\) whenever \(\eta\le2/(\mu+L)\). At the maximal step the norm factor is \((L-\mu)/(L+\mu)\). If \(L=\mu\), the shifted function is affine, and the \(1/L\) update reaches the minimizer in one step. [Nesterov, Introductory Lectures on Convex Programming, Theorems 2.1.13–2.1.14].

Nonconvex objectives

If \(f\) is smooth and bounded below, summing the decrease yields

\[\min_{0\le t<T}\|\nabla f(x_t)\|^2\le \frac{2(f(x_0)-\inf f)}{\eta T}.\]

This controls a first-order stationary point. For example, \(x^2-y^2+y^4\) is bounded below and has a stationary point at the origin, where the Hessian has one negative eigenvalue. A twice differentiable local minimizer has zero gradient and a positive semidefinite Hessian by restriction to lines. If its Hessian is positive definite, Taylor’s theorem gives a strict local minimum.

Full gradients for \(f=n^{-1}\sum_i f_i\) cost \(n\) component gradients. Uniform subsampling gives a cheaper unbiased direction whose variance and convergence properties are addressed in Lecture 3.

For a uniformly sampled subset of size \(b\) drawn without replacement, write \(a_i=\nabla f_i(x)-\nabla f(x)\) and \(\sigma_x^2=n^{-1}\sum_i\|a_i\|^2\). The sample-gradient error is \(b^{-1}\sum_i I_i a_i\). Since \(\mathbb E I_i=b/n\) and \(\mathbb E I_i I_j=b(b-1)/(n(n-1))\) for \(i\ne j\), its mean is zero and its variance is

\[\mathbb E\left\|b^{-1}\sum_iI_i a_i\right\|^2 =\frac{n-b}{b(n-1)}\sigma_x^2.\]

Here \(\sum_{i\ne j}\langle a_i,a_j\rangle=-\sum_i\|a_i\|^2\) because the centered gradients sum to zero. This calculation, for \(n\ge2\), reaches zero variance at a full batch.

Stochastic convex optimization and empirical minimizers

The course supplement shows how the smallest empirical objective can fail to identify a good population solution even for convex Lipschitz losses. Let \(w\) range over the Euclidean unit ball in \(\mathbb R^d\), let \(Z\) be a vector of independent fair bits, and use the convex 1-Lipschitz loss \(\|Z\odot w\|_2\). With \(d=2^n\), the chance that a coordinate is zero in all \(n\) independently drawn examples is \(2^{-n}\), independently across coordinates. Some coordinate is missing with probability \(1-(1-2^{-n})^{2^n}\ge1-e^{-1}\). Its unit basis vector then has empirical loss zero like \(w=0\), but population loss \(1/2\). Bad and good empirical minimizers coexist even though the population minimizer is known in this construction. The example rules out a distribution-free, dimension-independent guarantee for arbitrary empirical minimizers under these assumptions. Shalev-Shwartz et al., Stochastic Convex Optimization, §4.1.

The same example separates uniform estimation of risk from stochastic optimization. For a general convex Lipschitz loss \(\ell(w,Z)\) on a convex set, projected subgradient descent at iteration \(t\) uses a fresh observation \(Z_t\) and a subgradient \(g_t\in\partial_w\ell(w_t,Z_t)\) of norm at most \(L_\ell\). Squaring the projected distance to a fixed comparator \(u\) gives

\[\ell(w_t,Z_t)-\ell(u,Z_t) \le\frac{\|w_t-u\|^2-\|w_{t+1}-u\|^2}{2\eta}+\frac\eta2 L_\ell^2.\]

Independence gives \(\mathbb E[\ell(w_t,Z_t)\mid w_t]=F(w_t)\) because \(w_t\) is chosen before seeing \(Z_t\). Sum expectations and apply Jensen to the averaged weights for \(\mathbb E[F(\bar w_T)-F(u)]\le\|w_0-u\|^2/(2\eta T)+\eta L_\ell^2/2\). A step proportional to \(T^{-1/2}\) gives a dimension-independent population excess bound for this procedure. Shalev-Shwartz et al., §2.2, Theorem 3 and the online-to-batch discussion.

Challenges

Problem 1

For positive-definite quadratic \(f(x)=\tfrac12(x-x^\star)^\top A(x-x^\star)\) with eigenvalues in \([\mu,L]\), find the fixed step minimizing the worst-case one-step contraction in the \(A\)-norm.

Show solution

The squared \(A\)-norm is a weighted sum of squared eigencoordinates. Its norm contraction is bounded by \(\max_{\lambda\in[\mu,L]}|1-\eta\lambda|=\max\{|1-\eta\mu|,|1-\eta L|\}\). For \(\eta\in[1/L,1/\mu]\) the endpoints have opposite signs, so equality of their magnitudes gives \(\eta=2/(L+\mu)\). On either side of that balancing point the larger magnitude increases. The factor is \((L-\mu)/(L+\mu)\), and the function gap contracts by its square. The factor is attained by an extreme eigenvector if the endpoints are eigenvalues.

Problem 2

Suppose \(2\mu(f(x)-f^\star)\le\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\) and \(f\) is \(L\)-smooth. Prove a linear objective-gap rate and give a convex example satisfying the assumption without being strongly convex.

Show solution

The \(1/L\) step decreases \(f\) by at least \(\|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2L)\ge(\mu/L)(f(x)-f^\star)\). Iterate this inequality to get the factor \((1-\mu/L)^T\). The example \(f(x,y)=x^2/2\) gives equality with \(\mu=1\), while its line of minimizers precludes strong convexity.

Problem 3. Generalization from replacement stability

Let the per-example loss \(\ell(w,z)\) be \(\lambda\)-strongly convex and \(L_\ell\)-Lipschitz with respect to \(w\) on a convex set. Assume empirical and population minimizers exist. Prove that ERM satisfies \(\mathbb E[F(\widehat w)-F(w^\star)]\le2L_\ell^2/(\lambda n)\), then obtain a high-probability bound with failure probability \(\delta\). This sharpens the constant in the replacement-stability argument by using strong convexity twice. Shalev-Shwartz et al., Stochastic Convex Optimization, Theorem 6 and Claims 6.1–6.2.

Show solution

Write \(S^{(i)}\) for \(S\) with observation \(i\) independently replaced. Set \(w=\widehat w(S)\) and \(w'=\widehat w(S^{(i)})\). The two empirical objectives \(\widehat F_S,\widehat F_{S^{(i)}}\) are both \(\lambda\)-strongly convex. By constrained optimality, \(\widehat F_S(w')-\widehat F_S(w)\ge(\lambda/2)\|w-w'\|^2\) and \(\widehat F_{S^{(i)}}(w)-\widehat F_{S^{(i)}}(w')\ge(\lambda/2)\|w-w'\|^2\). Adding cancels all terms except observation \(i\). The two surviving differences are bounded by \(2L_\ell\|w-w'\|/n\). Therefore \(\|w-w'\|\le2L_\ell/(\lambda n)\) and any evaluation loss changes by at most \(\beta=2L_\ell^2/(\lambda n)\).

Because \(\widehat w(S^{(i)})\) is independent of the original \(Z_i\) and has the same distribution as \(\widehat w(S)\), \(\mathbb E F(\widehat w)=n^{-1}\sum_i\mathbb E\ell(\widehat w(S^{(i)}),Z_i)\). Subtracting the unmodified empirical score and applying stability gives \(\mathbb E[F(\widehat w)-\widehat F_S(\widehat w)]\le\beta\). Empirical optimality and unbiasedness at the fixed \(w^\star\) give \(\mathbb E\widehat F_S(\widehat w)\le\mathbb E\widehat F_S(w^\star)=F(w^\star)\). The population excess is nonnegative, so Markov bounds the probability that it exceeds \(\beta/\delta\) by \(\delta\).
