ML 13: Hyperparameter Optimization and Resource Allocation

Derive Gaussian-process posteriors, Expected Improvement, and hypergradients, then compare resource allocation under successive halving.

The synthetic example picks the unobserved location that maximizes Expected Improvement for a minimization problem.

Training and model selection

Lecture 13, slides 4–11 and 33.

The black-box setting allows discrete choices such as layer count and continuous choices such as learning rate. Each query can require a full training run. The lecture’s egg example has the same structure: the quality measurement comes after trying a combination of boiling time, cooling time, and seasoning. Grid search fixes a schedule, random search draws configurations independently, and Bayesian optimization conditions its next query on earlier outcomes. Hypergradients require a differentiable dependence on the hyperparameter. Bandit allocation can exploit cheaper partial evaluations.

The inner procedure produces weights \(w_T(\lambda)\) for hyperparameters \(\lambda\). The outer optimization minimizes \(F(\lambda)=L_{\rm val}(w_T(\lambda),\lambda)\). Fixed training time and full convergence can rank configurations differently. Random seeds make single runs noisy, so comparisons can use common seeds or repeated runs. Keep the final test set separate from the choice of hyperparameters.

A grid with \(m\) values per dimension uses \(m^d\) evaluations. If one coordinate dominates, it tests just \(m\) distinct values for that coordinate. Sampling \(m^d\) configurations from a continuous distribution generally tests a different value each time along that coordinate. Distributions on positive scales that span orders of magnitude are often defined in log space.

Gaussian-process prediction

Lecture 13, slides 12 and 14–20. Rasmussen and Williams, Chapter 2.

A Gaussian process is a consistent prior on function values: for any finite list of inputs, the corresponding values form a joint Gaussian vector, with mean given by the mean function and covariance given by the kernel. This finite-vector statement is enough for prediction.

Let \(F\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)\) and observe \(y_j=F(\lambda_j)+e_j\), where independent \(e_j\sim N(0,\sigma_n^2)\). Define \(K_{ij}=k(\lambda_i,\lambda_j)\), \(\Sigma=K+\sigma_n^2I\), and \(k_\star=(k(\lambda_i,\lambda_\star))_i\). Consider the residual

\[r=F(\lambda_\star)-m(\lambda_\star)-k_\star^\top\Sigma^{-1}(y-m_\Lambda).\]

The cross-covariance of \(F(\lambda_\star)\) with \(y\) is \(k_\star^\top\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(y)=\Sigma\). Hence \(\operatorname{Cov}(r,y)=k_\star^\top-k_\star^\top\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma=0\). The jointly Gaussian pair \((r,y)\) has a block-diagonal covariance matrix, so its joint characteristic function factors and \(r\) is independent of \(y\). The residual variance expansion subtracts \(2k_\star^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_\star\) through the cross term and adds \(k_\star^\top\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}k_\star\) through the predictor’s variance. This yields

\[\mu_\star=m(\lambda_\star)+k_\star^\top\Sigma^{-1}(y-m_\Lambda),\qquad s_\star^2=k(\lambda_\star,\lambda_\star)-k_\star^\top\Sigma^{-1}k_\star.\]

To predict a future noisy observation, add \(\sigma_n^2\) to the latent variance.

Expected Improvement

For a reference value \(b\) to beat in minimization, define \(\mathrm{EI}=\mathbb E[(b-F)_+]\). Write \(F=\mu+s Z\) for standard normal \(Z\), and set \(z=(b-\mu)/s\). The integral is

\[\mathrm{EI}=\int_{-\infty}^z(b-\mu-su)\phi(u)\,du =(b-\mu)\Phi(z)+s\phi(z),\]

because \(\phi'(u)=-u\phi(u)\). When \(s=0\), the limit is \((b-\mu)_+\). Low mean and high uncertainty can both make a point worth evaluating. Noisy evaluations may favor basing \(b\) on predicted latent values.

The successive plots on slides 14–20 show this closed loop: evaluate the maximizer of the current acquisition function, insert the observation into the GP, then recompute the mean, uncertainty, and acquisition. Low posterior variance near evaluated points makes large improvement there less likely. Sequential conditioning is the reason the simplest version needs a different acquisition rule for parallel batches.

Differentiating through the training trajectory

Lecture 13, slides 21–31. Maclaurin, Duvenaud, and Adams, Sections 2.1–2.3.

For learning rate \(\eta\) and \(w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\nabla L_{\rm train}(w_t)\), assume \(w_0\) is independent of \(\eta\). Differentiating both the multiplication by \(\eta\) and the gradient’s input gives

\[u_{t+1}=(I-\eta H_t)u_t-\nabla L_{\rm train}(w_t),\qquad u_t=\partial_\eta w_t,\quad H_t=\nabla^2 L_{\rm train}(w_t),\quad u_0=0.\]

The outer derivative is \(\nabla_wL_{\rm val}(w_T,\eta)^\top u_T+\partial_\eta L_{\rm val}(w_T,\eta)\). For the slides’ two-step least-squares example \(L(w)=\|Xw-y\|^2/2\), let \(g_t=X^\top(Xw_t-y)\) and \(H=X^\top X\). Then \(u_1=-g_0\), so \(u_2=-g_0-g_1+\eta Hg_0\). If the outer objective is \(L(w_2)\) as in the example, the two-step hypergradient is \(g_2^\top(-g_0-g_1+\eta Hg_0)\).

Reconstructing momentum SGD and reversing its derivatives

Use the fresh velocity in the position update. This convention is consistent with reversing the position before recomputing its gradient. Write \(g_t=\nabla_w L_t(w_t,\theta)\), where \(t\) also fixes the batch and other training inputs. The forward map is

\[v_{t+1}=\gamma_t v_t-(1-\gamma_t)g_t,\qquad w_{t+1}=w_t+\alpha_t v_{t+1}.\]

The update for \(v_{t+1}\) contains an exponentially weighted history of gradients. To recover the previous state in exact arithmetic, compute \(w_t=w_{t+1}-\alpha_t v_{t+1}\), recompute \(g_t\) at \(w_t\), and recover \(v_t=(v_{t+1}+(1-\gamma_t)g_t)/\gamma_t\) for \(\gamma_t\ne0\).

Let \(a=\partial F/\partial w_{t+1}\) and \(b=\partial F/\partial v_{t+1}\) be column adjoints arriving from future steps. The position update contributes \(a^\top v_{t+1}\) to the derivative with respect to \(\alpha_t\), contributes \(a\) directly to the adjoint for \(w_t\), and changes the velocity adjoint to \(\widetilde b=b+\alpha_t a\). Differentiating the first equation gives the remaining updates:

\[\begin{aligned} \partial_{\gamma_t}F&\mathrel{+}=\widetilde b^\top(v_t+g_t),\\ \partial_\theta F&\mathrel{+}=-(1-\gamma_t)(D_\theta g_t)^\top\widetilde b,\\ a_t&=a-(1-\gamma_t)(D_w g_t)^\top\widetilde b,\qquad b_t=\gamma_t\widetilde b. \end{aligned}\]

Initialize with \(a_T=\nabla_w F(w_T)\) and \(b_T=0\) if \(F\) is independent of the final velocity. For shared hyperparameters, sum their contributions over steps. In particular, \((D_w g_t)^\top\widetilde b\) is a Hessian-vector product obtained by differentiating \(g_t^\top\widetilde b\) at fixed \(\widetilde b\). A full Hessian is unnecessary.

Reversible fixed-point momentum decay

Floating-point rounding breaks the exact inverse. Even when only a velocity rounding error is considered, a reverse decay multiplies it by \(1/\gamma_t\). Repeating this for constant \(0<\gamma<1\) can magnify an existing error by \(\gamma^{-T}\).

In the paper’s fixed-point scheme, let \(c\) be an integer encoding a velocity coordinate, \(B\ge0\) an information buffer, and \(\gamma=n/d\) with positive integers \(n<d\). Euclidean division writes \(c=dq+r\) with \(0\le r<d\). Put \(S=dB+r\), and divide it as \(S=nB'+a\) with \(0\le a<n\). The new pair is \((c',B')=(nq+a,B')\). Inverting, first divide \(c'\) by \(n\) to recover \(q,a\). Then recover \(S=nB'+a\), divide by \(d\) to get \(B,r\), and set \(c=dq+r\). The final digit \(a\) uses the space left by multiplying \(q\) by \(n\). It frees up roughly \(\log_2 n\) bits per step compared with storing the remainder without this transfer.

For constant \(n/d\), \(B'\le(d/n)B+(d-1)/n\). Iterating that inequality gives \(B_T=O((d/n)^T)\), so the buffer needs at most \(T\log_2(d/n)+O(1)\) bits for an integer coordinate, up to the fixed initial/setup cost. At \(\gamma=0.98\), \(\log_2(1/\gamma)\approx0.0291\), compared with 32 bits for a full per-step snapshot of one coordinate. Practical overhead also depends on integer representation, overflow management, and whether the same gradient and batch can be reconstructed. The bit logic reverses the chosen discrete arithmetic. The adjoint equations describe the underlying real-valued training updates.

Successive halving and Hyperband

Lecture 13, slides 34–41. Jamieson and Talwalkar, Algorithm 1, and Li et al., Section 2.

The fixed-budget stationary version in the slides begins with \(S_0=[n]\), spends approximately \(B/\log_2 n\) pulls each round, and divides those pulls equally among active arms. It then retains the half with largest empirical reward. For eight arms, slides 36–41 show the first round retaining A3, A5, A6, and A7, the second retaining A5 and A7, and the third selecting A7. The shown fourth value is a check of the final arm, since the elimination algorithm terminates when one arm remains. Allocation in training uses resource levels rather than repeated draws from a fixed reward distribution.

Start \(n\) candidates with \(r\) units of resource. Rank their scores, retain roughly a fraction \(1/\eta_{\mathrm{sh}}\), and multiply the resource per survivor by \(\eta_{\mathrm{sh}}\). With 16 candidates, resource 1, and elimination factor 2, the round totals from scratch are \(16,16,16,16,16\) epochs. Continuing from checkpoints makes the increments \(16,8,8,8,8\), totaling 48 epochs. The saving assumes earlier training can be reused. Learning curves that cross can eliminate a slow-starting eventual winner. Hyperband distributes its budget among brackets with different initial candidate counts and starting budgets.

In a stationary best-arm model, each configuration \(i\) has reward mean \(\mu_i\). If every arm is sampled \(m\) times with independent rewards in \([0,1]\) and the unique best mean exceeds the others by at least \(\Delta\), Hoeffding bounds the chance of any empirical mean deviating by at least \(\Delta/2\) by \(2n e^{-m\Delta^2/2}\). On the complementary event the empirical best is the genuine best. The same argument yields a conservative guarantee for the slide’s fixed-budget elimination scheme with fresh independent samples. At round \(r\), condition on the entire prior history and on the unique best arm surviving. With \(m_r\) new samples per remaining arm and gap at least \(\Delta\), the probability of any current estimate deviating by at least \(\Delta/2\) is at most \(2|S_r|e^{-m_r\Delta^2/2}\) by a union bound. Otherwise the true best has a strictly higher estimate than every other arm and survives. Sum this bound over the possible round of its first elimination. This bound does not require independence across survival events, only fresh samples within the round. Early-training scores generally evolve with allocation, so a stationary gap guarantee does not transfer automatically.

Challenge

Let the inner objective be \(L_{\rm train}(w,\lambda)=\tfrac12(w-a)^2+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}w^2\) and the outer objective \(\tfrac12(w^\star(\lambda)-b)^2\), with \(a>b>0\) and \(\lambda\ge0\). Derive the exact minimizing regularization and verify the derivative by implicit differentiation.

Show solution

Stationarity is \((1+\lambda)w^\star=a\), so \(w^\star=a/(1+\lambda)\). It decreases from \(a\) to 0, so the squared loss is minimized when \(w^\star=b\), at \(\lambda=a/b-1\). Implicit differentiation of stationarity gives \((1+\lambda)\partial_\lambda w^\star+w^\star=0\). The hypergradient is \(-(w^\star-b)w^\star/(1+\lambda)\), negative below the optimum and positive above it.

Second challenge. Show that Expected Improvement is nondecreasing in predictive standard deviation when the mean and current best are fixed.

Show solution to the second challenge

Write \(a\) for the incumbent minus the mean in minimization, \(z=a/s\), and \(\mathrm{EI}(s)=a\Phi(z)+s\phi(z)\). Differentiate for \(s>0\). The derivatives through \(z\) cancel because \(\phi'(z)=-z\phi(z)\) and \(a=sz\). Hence \(\mathrm{EI}'(s)=\phi(z)>0\). Continuity at \(s=0\) gives \(\max(a,0)\), so uncertainty increases this acquisition value even at fixed predicted mean.

Research challenge — GP-UCB regret from information gain

Let \(D\) be a finite domain of \(N\) configurations, and maximize \(f\) drawn from a known GP with \(k(x,x)\le1\). At step \(t\) observe \(y_t=f(x_t)+\epsilon_t\), with independent \(\epsilon_t\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\), \(\sigma^2>0\). Let \(x_t\) maximize \(\mu_{t-1}(x)+\sqrt{\beta_t}s_{t-1}(x)\), with \(\beta_t=2\log(N\pi^2t^2/(3\delta))\) for \(0<\delta<1\). Define

\[\gamma_T=\max_{x_{1:T}\in D^T}\tfrac12\log\det(I+\sigma^{-2}K_T).\]

Here \(K_T\) is the covariance matrix at a list of \(T\) points and repeats are allowed. Prove that with probability at least \(1-\delta\), the cumulative regret satisfies

\[\sum_{t=1}^T[f(x^\star)-f(x_t)] \le\sqrt{\frac{8T\beta_T\gamma_T}{\log(1+\sigma^{-2})}}\]

simultaneously for every \(T\ge1\).

Show research challenge solution

Conditional on the history, the residual at each fixed \(x\) has standard deviation \(s_{t-1}(x)\). When this is positive, a Gaussian tail bounds violation of the interval \(\mu_{t-1}(x)\pm\sqrt{\beta_t}s_{t-1}(x)\) by \(2e^{-\beta_t/2}\). At zero variance the interval is exact. This conditional bound also holds unconditionally. A union bound over \(D\) and all \(t\) sums to \(\sum_t 6\delta/(\pi^2t^2)=\delta\). On the common event, optimism gives \(f(x^\star)\le\mu_{t-1}(x_t)+\sqrt{\beta_t}s_{t-1}(x_t)\) and \(f(x_t)\ge\mu_{t-1}(x_t)-\sqrt{\beta_t}s_{t-1}(x_t)\). The instantaneous regret is at most \(2\sqrt{\beta_t}s_{t-1}(x_t)\).

Fix any realized list of query locations when forming its covariance matrix. Successive Schur complements of the noisy covariance \(K_T+\sigma^2 I\) give

\[\log\det(I+\sigma^{-2}K_T) =\sum_{t=1}^T\log(1+\sigma^{-2}s_{t-1}^2(x_t)).\]

This is a deterministic matrix identity at each list, even though the list came from a random adaptive procedure. For a fixed list, half the determinant logarithm is also the mutual information between its latent values and observations, by subtracting the entropy of the independent noise from the entropy of the observations. Since \(s_{t-1}^2(x_t)\in[0,1]\), concavity of \(q\mapsto\log(1+\sigma^{-2}q)\) places it above the chord \(q\log(1+\sigma^{-2})\). Thus the sum of posterior variances is at most \(2\gamma_T/\log(1+\sigma^{-2})\). Cauchy–Schwarz now bounds the squared cumulative regret by \(4T\beta_T\sum_t s_{t-1}^2(x_t)\), proving the claim. Minimization follows by using a lower confidence bound or applying this result to \(-f\).
